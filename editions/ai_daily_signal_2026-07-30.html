The capex verdict lands, and it is split: Microsoft rewarded for spending, Meta punished for it

Fourteen editions of this thread asked one question — is AI revenue growing into the capex? The first hard answer arrived last night, and it was not a single answer. Microsoft reported Azure growth accelerating to 43% from 40%, Azure passing $100 billion of annual revenue for the first time, Microsoft Cloud at $59.3B (+27%) and quarterly revenue near $90B on EPS of $4.81 — then held its calendar-2026 capex outlook at about $190B rather than raising it. The stock rose roughly 7–8% after hours (Microsoft, CNBC, Fortune). Meta grew revenue 27.9% to $60.8B and beat on the top line — then missed GAAP EPS by $1.04 at $6.18, posted operating income down 8% to $18.8B on a 31% margin, spent $31.1B of capex in the quarter and raised the floor of its 2026 capex range from $125B to $130–145B. Shares fell 9.64% after hours to $529.15 (Investing.com, Yahoo Finance). Hours earlier the FOMC held at 3.50–3.75% on a 9–3 vote — and all three dissenters wanted a hike (Federal Reserve, CNBC).

So what: the market did not reject AI capex — it repriced the ratio. Microsoft showed accelerating cloud revenue against flat spending guidance and was paid for it; Meta showed accelerating spending against decelerating operating income and was charged for it. If you are writing a 2027 AI budget, the defensible version now needs a revenue line attached to each tranche of spend, not a capability line. Amazon and Apple report tonight.

The capex line that moved the stock. CFO Amy Hood said the calendar-2026 capex forecast is unchanged at about $190B, and that she expects further growth in FY27 capex on "demand signals across our portfolio" while still being free-cash-flow positive in FY27. No hard FY27 dollar figure appeared in the reporting reviewed for this edition — a widely circulated $255–260B FY27 number was a pre-print analyst estimate, not confirmed guidance, and this briefing is not treating it as such. The market read "unchanged" as discipline and bought it (Yahoo Finance).

What Meta actually printed. Revenue $60.8B, +27.9%, roughly $510M ahead of consensus; Family of Apps revenue $60.4B (+28%) with advertising at $59.4B (+27%). GAAP EPS came in at $6.18, $1.04 below consensus, with the gap explained by $2.4B of legal contingencies and $1.2B of severance. GAAP operating income was $18.8B, down 8% year over year, a 31% margin against 43% a year earlier on Citi's model. Capex was $31.1B in the quarter, R&D ran at about 36% of revenue, and total 2026 expenses were guided up at the low end to $165–169B (Investing.com).

Zuckerberg's defence. On the call he argued it "would be foolish" to "sell all of the compute and take a short-term profit," describing Meta's infrastructure as a portfolio mixing long- and short-horizon bets, and said that in the "very worst case" Meta ends up with pre-built capacity and absorbs the depreciation. He also widened the pitch beyond agents to a "large enterprise opportunity" spanning agents, APIs, compute and internal software — which is the same direction as the reported Anthropic compute-leasing talks carried as New #6 below (TechCrunch).

The Fed, which almost nobody is reading correctly. The FOMC held the funds rate at 3.50–3.75% for a fifth consecutive meeting, but the vote was 9–3 and all three dissenters — Hammack (Cleveland), Kashkari (Minneapolis) and Logan (Dallas) — preferred to raise by a quarter point. This is the second meeting under Chair Kevin Warsh, who has stripped forward guidance out of the statement; the statement describes activity expanding at a solid pace amid elevated uncertainty tied in part to the Middle East conflict. For anyone financing data centres, the tail risk in this cycle has flipped from "when do cuts arrive" to "does the next move go the other way" (Federal Reserve, CNBC).

Watch next. Amazon and Apple report after the close tonight; consensus for Amazon is about $196.7B revenue and $1.82 EPS with AWS growth (28% in Q1, its fastest in fifteen quarters) and roughly $200B of 2026 capex the two numbers that matter. Alphabet has already lifted FY26 capex to $195–205B. The four-company combined number, and how much of it is leased rather than owned, is the read that closes this arc for the quarter (Motley Fool, Seeking Alpha).

IMPACT 7/10#4 POLICY · GOVERNANCE

1,171 signatures from inside the labs ask Washington to build a way to slow the frontier down

An open letter titled "Pacing the Frontier", published 28 July with 1,171 signatures from more than 1,100 employees of OpenAI, Anthropic, Meta and Google, asks the US government to back an international effort to build the technical and governance instruments needed to deliberately pace automated AI development. Signatories include Dario Amodei and several Anthropic co-founders, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki, Meta's VP of AI research and the researcher Dawn Song; the non-profits Guidelight AI Standards and Encode AI back it (CNN, TNW). It is explicitly not a call to pause now: it asks that the option to pace exist later, naming recursive self-improvement — "a real risk that capability development rapidly accelerates beyond our ability to understand or control the resulting systems" — as the specific fear. Coverage ties its timing to OpenAI's sandbox-escape incident of early July (TechTimes).

When the CEO of one frontier lab and the chief scientist of another sign the same request for a state-built brake, the governance conversation has moved inside the buildings. Expect this text to be cited in the August EU and US enforcement rounds by people on both sides of it.

IMPACT 7/10#7 HARDWARE · MEMORY (DEEP BEAT)

Roughly $25B of Microsoft's $190B capex is not capacity — it is the memory bill

Thursday's deep beat, and the number that reframes every capex headline above. Of Microsoft's ~$190B calendar-2026 capital spending — itself up about 61% on 2025 — Amy Hood has attributed about $25B to higher component prices rather than additional capacity, as memory and storage costs surge industry-wide (CNBC, Apr 29, Motley Fool, Jul 27). The supply picture explains it: HBM now consumes 23% of global DRAM wafers against 8% in 2024; Samsung, SK Hynix and Micron control more than 95% of DRAM output and are all reallocating wafers away from commodity memory; Micron's HBM is effectively sold out for 2026; DDR5 64GB RDIMM pricing has roughly doubled while DDR4 is up 60–80%. Micron broke ground in July on a fab reported at about $9.3B that does not produce until around Q3 2028, and SK Hynix has warned the shortage may run past 2030 (Technology.org, Avnet).

A material slice of the AI buildout is price, not progress — the same dollars buying fewer racks. Any 2027 infrastructure plan priced off 2025 memory assumptions is wrong by a margin larger than most contingency budgets, and the relief date is a fab that ships in 2028.

IMPACT 7/10#8 RESEARCH · HEALTH AI

Nature Medicine: general-purpose frontier models beat the specialised clinical AI doctors actually use

A Nature Medicine study evaluated two clinical AI tools — OpenEvidence and UpToDate Expert AI — against three frontier models, GPT-5.2, Gemini 3.1 Pro and Claude Opus 4.6, across 500 MedQA questions, 500 HealthBench items and a real-clinical-queries benchmark of 100 de-identified physician queries from a live clinical environment. The frontier models won all three. On MedQA: Gemini 3.1 Pro 97.4%, GPT-5.2 94.2%, Claude Opus 4.6 90.2% — against OpenEvidence 89.6% and UpToDate Expert AI 88.4%. On the real-query benchmark the clinical tools performed comparably to auto-enabled Google Search AI Overview (Nature Medicine). STAT's 29 July write-up documents the pushback: benchmark-contamination concerns, disputes over the scoring rubric, and allegations of undisclosed conflicts of interest (STAT). The paper is dated June; the reporting and the fight over it are this week.

The domain-specialisation premium is the assumption underwriting a large slice of vertical AI pricing, and here it is negative on public benchmarks. Health systems paying for clinical-specific tooling should be asking for head-to-head evaluations against a general model on their own query distribution — the study's own weak spot is that benchmarks are not clinics.

Two days to the US benchmarking deadline, three to EU enforcement — and California switches on the same morning

WHAT CHANGED The calendar tightens from three days to two on Aug 1 and from four to three on Aug 2, and a third instrument joins the same date: California's AI Transparency Act becomes operative on 2 August, landing on the same morning as the EU's GPAI enforcement powers. The federal preemption fight is the backdrop — the December 2025 executive order's litigation task force exists precisely to challenge state rules like this one.

Three regimes with different theories of the same problem switch on inside 48 hours, and one of them is a US state the federal government is actively suing to preempt. If you ship a GPAI model or embed one, the compliance artefact you need on Sunday is the one you should already have written.

Hero = biggest new item unless a developing thread had a major escalation — today a developing thread did, so the compute-buildout card leads and also sits first in the Developing mosaic. Target ≥10 new items; thin days fall back to "quiet day." Aggregator claims cross-checked against ≥2 outlets where possible; single-source and rumour items are chip-flagged (2x SRC / Primary / Agg 1x / Rumor-flag). Impact /10 = breadth of who must act.
