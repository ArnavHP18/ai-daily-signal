Alibaba puts its biggest model on the table and promises the weights next week

On Monday 3 August Alibaba unveiled Qwen3.8-Max, the largest and — by its own account — most capable model the Qwen team has built, and opened API access to developers worldwide with the statement that the weights will be published for download next week on Hugging Face and ModelScope (Alizila, SCMP, Quartz). The architecture is a mixture-of-experts at 2.4 trillion total parameters with 95 billion active per query, a 1M-token context, 65,536 default output tokens expandable to 131,072, and text-plus-image input (MarkTechPost, The Decoder). The number that matters is not the parameter count. It is the tier: Max is Alibaba's closed tier, and this is the first Max-class Qwen whose weights are to be released — a reversal of the posture Alibaba adopted earlier this year when it kept several flagship releases proprietary while continuing to open the smaller families. Pricing is deliberately aggressive: Alibaba puts international rates at roughly 40% of Claude Opus 5 on input and 24% on output, with the preview sold at 10% of standard through its Token Plan subscription and its Qoder coding products, while third-party gateways list about $2/$6 per million (OpenRouter, Dataconomy).

So what: three things are true and only two are verified. Qwen3.8-Max exists and is callable today — that is verified. Its pricing undercuts the Western top tier by roughly 3–4x — that is verified from published rates. That it is "competitive with leading models from OpenAI and Anthropic" is Alibaba's own claim with no independent benchmark surfaced in this scan, and the open weights are a promise dated next week, not an artefact you can download. Treat this as a procurement event with a fuse on it: if the weights land as stated, the top of the open-weight tier moves from Moonshot's 2.8T Kimi K3 to a second 2T-plus model inside three weeks, and any build-vs-buy model that assumed the closed labs owned the frontier price floor needs re-running. If they slip, this was a preview.

IMPACT 8/10#2 POLICY · EU

The EU's real marking deadline is 2 December — and the same date bans nudification tools

Regulation (EU) 2026/1744, the Digital Omnibus on AI, entered full legal force on 27 July 2026, three days after publication in the Official Journal (TechTimes, Data Protection Report). Three things it does that change the compliance calendar this briefing has been carrying. One: AI systems placed on the EU market before 2 August 2026 get a four-month grace period, to 2 December 2026, before the Article 50(2) machine-readable-marking obligation applies to them. Two: the grace period for providers to implement transparency solutions for artificially generated content was cut from six months to three — landing on the same 2 December date. Three: systems that generate CSAM, or images, video or audio depicting an identifiable person's intimate parts or sexually explicit activity without consent, may not be placed on the EU market absent adequate technical safeguards, with compliance required by 2 December 2026 (Law Society Gazette, ComplexDiscovery). Separately the omnibus fixes high-risk application dates at 2 December 2027 for stand-alone systems and 2 August 2028 for those embedded in products (Gibson Dunn, Council of the EU).

This corrects a framing this briefing carried yesterday. Article 50 switching on for new placements on 2 August is not the same as every deployed generator having to mark output that day — the installed base has until 2 December, which is why California's SB 942 and the EU regime are not actually in lockstep. If you shipped before 2 August you have four months; if you ship after, you do not. Put 2 December on the compliance calendar as the single date that carries marking, the transparency implementation deadline and the nudification ban at once.

IMPACT 7/10#3 POLICY · CHINA

China's first national framework written specifically for AI agents is now enforceable

The Implementation Opinions on the Standardized Application and Innovative Development of Intelligent Agents, issued jointly by the CAC, NDRC and MIIT on 8 May 2026, became enforceable on 15 July (NYU Shanghai RITS, MMLC Group). It is the first national policy framework anywhere dedicated to agents as a category rather than to generative AI generally: it defines an agent as a system capable of autonomous perception, memory, decision-making, interaction and execution, which pulls agents out of the bucket governed by China's 2023 generative-AI rules on the reasoning that autonomy creates distinct risks. The document is built on four pillars — development foundation, safety bottom line, application uptake, innovation ecosystem — and names 19 application scenarios across research, industry, consumption, public welfare and governance. The operative part for anyone shipping: agents used in healthcare, transport, media and public safety face mandatory filing, compliance testing and product-recall provisions, and the text names manipulative behaviour, emotional dependency, privacy leakage, system failure and false-information generation as the risks to be controlled (Rimon Law, HealthTechAsia).

A product-recall provision for software agents is the novel instrument here — it treats a misbehaving agent like a defective physical good, not like a content-moderation failure. If you operate agents in China in any of the four named sectors, the filing and testing duty is already live and has been for three weeks. Everyone else should read the 19 scenarios as the clearest published statement of what one major government thinks agents are actually for.

IMPACT 6/10#5 POLICY · WORKFORCE

Gallup's counterweight: only 1% of laid-off workers name AI as the reason they lost the job

Gallup surveyed 23,717 US employees between 4 and 19 February 2026 and published findings that cut against the headline layoff attribution this briefing has been carrying (Gallup). Two results sit in tension. First, AI non-users were 62% of laid-off workers against 50% of the currently employed, and among tech employees, those who rarely use AI carry an 18% predicted probability of layoff versus 6% for regular users — a threefold gap (Boston Globe). Second, when laid-off workers were asked why, only 1% named AI or automation as the primary reason; the top answers were organisational restructuring (15%), budget and cost cutting (11%) and economic conditions (11%) (Fox Business). Note the dating honestly: this is a February field period reported publicly in mid-June, not a fresh read.

Two measurement systems are describing the same labour market and disagreeing about causation, not about facts. Challenger counts what employers announce as the reason — 101,743 AI-cited cuts in 2026. Gallup asks what workers experienced — 1%. Both are real; "AI-attributed" is an employer speech act, and employers have reasons to use it. The defensible operational reading is Gallup's other finding: non-use of AI correlates with being cut, whatever the stated cause.

IMPACT 5/10#9 RESEARCH · RETRIEVAL

AskChem makes the retrievable unit a claim with a DOI attached, not a paper

The week's top trending paper on Hugging Face as of 2 August is AskChem (arXiv 2607.28618, posted 28 July), which argues that literature search returns ranked document lists when what a scientist or an agent actually needs is a specific finding with its provenance intact. It converts each paper into atomic, typed claims, every one grounded by a source DOI plus a verbatim quote or an explicit evidence locator, and exposes a stabilised faceted taxonomy, an evidence graph linking claims by relation, and an exploratory living taxonomy. Scale: 2.4 million searchable claims extracted from 147,000 papers, which the authors say makes contradictions between papers surfaceable rather than buried. The reported effect on a reader model is the number to note — grounding GPT-5.5 in AskChem yields 100% resolvable DOIs against 88.3% without retrieval, and the highest citation density among five systems tested (HF paper page, askchem.org).

An 11.7-point gap in resolvable citations is a direct measurement of how often an ungrounded frontier model cites something that does not exist. If you are building any research or evidence-synthesis agent, the transferable design is the unit of retrieval — claims carry provenance, documents do not.

IMPACT 7/10D3 POLICY · PROVENANCE

California is enforcing now; Brussels mostly is not until December

WHAT CHANGED De-escalated on the EU leg, unchanged on the California leg. Yesterday's card framed Sacramento and Brussels as switching marking obligations on within a day of each other. The Digital Omnibus (New #2) gives EU systems placed before 2 August a four-month grace to 2 December on Article 50(2), so the two regimes are not in lockstep — California's per-day penalties are live against the installed base and the EU's are not.
SB 942 as amended by AB 853 became operative on 2 August 2026: any generative-AI image, video or audio system with more than one million monthly California users must embed machine-readable provenance carrying provider name, system name and version, creation timestamp and a unique content identifier in a C2PA-readable format that survives ordinary editing, must offer a free public detection tool, and must let users attach a visible AI label — with civil penalties starting at $5,000 per violation per day. AB 853 was signed on 13 October 2025 specifically to move the operative date onto the EU milestone. Operative-day compliance was already reported as uneven, with TechTimes noting Midjourney outputs carrying no watermark as penalties began. What today's omnibus reading adds is that the intended symmetry did not survive contact with the EU's own transition rules.

If you deferred marking work on the theory that both regimes started together and enforcement would be slow on both, the exposure is asymmetric: California's clock is running on your existing product today, and the EU's runs from 2 December for anything you shipped before 2 August. Sequence the work Californian-first.
