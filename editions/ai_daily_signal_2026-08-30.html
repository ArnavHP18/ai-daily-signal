Amazon is closing Mechanical Turk, the human workforce it sold as artificial intelligence, after 21 years

Amazon will shut Amazon Mechanical Turk on 30 September 2026, ending the crowdsourced task marketplace it launched in 2005 and which at its peak carried more than 500,000 registered workers. New customer signups already closed on 30 July 2026, and two adjacent services go with it: AWS SageMaker Ground Truth and Amazon Augmented AI (A2I) — the two products that packaged human labelling and human review directly into the machine-learning pipeline. Amazon's stated reason is an internal assessment of its programs and services; it has not attributed the closure to AI (CNBC, 25 Aug, Quartz, 26 Aug, TechTimes, 26 Aug, TNW). The name was always the joke and the thesis at once: Jeff Bezos called it "artificial artificial intelligence" — a machine interface with people behind it, after the 18th-century chess automaton with a man inside.

So what: this is the first large, dated retirement of the human layer that made modern machine learning possible, and it closes with two uncomfortable facts attached. The demand did not vanish — it moved to Scale AI, Mercor and Prolific, who recruit better-paid, better-vetted specialists rather than an open pool of anonymous micro-taskers. And the pool itself had already been contaminated by the thing it was training: a 2023 study by Swiss researchers found up to 46% of MTurk workers were using large language models to complete their assigned tasks. A marketplace built to supply human judgement to machines was, by the end, partly machines supplying judgement to machines. If you run evaluations, annotation or human-in-the-loop review on MTurk, Ground Truth or A2I, you have 31 days and a vendor migration you did not plan for.

MTurk is a dated, primary, irreversible corporate action with a countdown on it. It leads on those grounds, at new24h:false, with 25 August on its face.

What actually shuts, and when. Three things, staggered. New customer signups for MTurk, SageMaker Ground Truth and Amazon Augmented A2I closed on 30 July 2026. The marketplace itself stops on 30 September 2026. Amazon's public framing is an internal review of programs and services — the same language it has used for other service sunsets — and it has pointedly not said "because AI can do this now." That restraint is worth noting rather than talking over: the causal story here is being supplied by commentators, not by Amazon.

The honest causal read. Two forces, not one. The first is substitution at the low end: tasks that were worth $0.05 to a human in 2015 — image tagging, sentiment labels, transcript cleanup — are now cheaper to run through a model. The second is a quality collapse that predates the substitution. The 2023 Swiss finding that up to 46% of MTurk workers were themselves using LLMs to complete tasks means the platform's core product — independent human judgement — was already compromised as a research instrument. Academic social science had been quietly migrating off MTurk for years for exactly this reason. The shutdown is the end of a decline, not a sudden displacement event.

Where the work went. Not away. Scale AI, Mercor and Prolific now sit where MTurk did, and the shape of the labour changed with the address: fewer anonymous micro-taskers on cents-per-task, more vetted domain specialists — doctors, lawyers, competitive programmers — paid hourly rates to produce and grade expert data for post-training. The AI labour market did not shrink at the human end; it stratified. That is the version of "AI takes jobs" that shows up in this briefing's ledger far more often than outright elimination, and it is exactly the shift today's Developing #3 measures at 365 events and 209,032 workers.

What to do this week, concretely. (1) Inventory every pipeline that calls MTurk, Ground Truth or A2I — including the forgotten ones inside research and QA teams — because 30 September is 31 days out and there is no extension on offer. (2) If you use it for evaluation rather than annotation, do not port the design straight across: the 46% contamination finding applies retroactively to any benchmark or human-preference baseline you collected there, and re-basing is cheaper than defending a stale number later. (3) Price the replacement honestly. Vetted-specialist platforms are not a cheaper MTurk; they are a different cost structure, and budgets set against micro-task pricing will not survive contact.

The line worth keeping. Mechanical Turk was named for a machine that pretended to be intelligent while a human hid inside. It is being retired in the year the machines stopped needing the human — and the last recorded thing many of those humans did on the platform was quietly paste the work into a chatbot.

IMPACT 7/10#2 HARDWARE · MEMORY

Google Play writes a memory ceiling for every Android app, because AI data centres took the DRAM

On 27 August Google published two new Play app-quality requirements, the load-bearing one being memory. From February 2027, apps must meet new thresholds for dynamic memory usage, bitmap usage and code optimisation, with developers expected to shrink and optimise code by at least 25%. Apps that miss the thresholds are not banned — they lose Play Store visibility and publishing capabilities, which for most publishers is the same thing with extra steps. The cause is stated openly in the coverage: Samsung, SK hynix and Micron are allocating fab capacity to higher-margin HBM for AI accelerators rather than commodity mobile DRAM. The price move is the evidence — flagship LPDDR5/LPDDR5X went from roughly $3.50–$5.50 per GB on bulk contracts in mid-2025 to $19.00–$21.00 per GB this year, a four-to-six-fold rise (TechCrunch, The Register, Tom's Hardware).

This briefing has carried the memory constraint on dram-hbm-supercycle as a supplier and hyperscaler story — SK hynix's "critical global HBM shortage", NVIDIA's 15%-plus 2027 server price notice, TrendForce's 2027 bit-growth forecast. Today it becomes a compliance deadline for people who do not build AI at all. A mobile developer in Pune or Warsaw with no AI in the product now has a dated engineering obligation created entirely by accelerator demand, and the cheaper phones at the bottom of the market are where it lands hardest. This is the first consumer-side invoice for the buildout, and it will not be the last.
