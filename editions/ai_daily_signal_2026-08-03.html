OpenAI names its next model by handing mathematicians ten proofs they can check without trusting OpenAI

On 1 August OpenAI published Ten advances in mathematics and theoretical computer science — and disclosed midway through it that the results came from an internal version of Astra, the company's next major model, which has not been released (OpenAI, Techmeme). The ten problems had seen no progress on the main result for at least a decade each, and span high-dimensional geometry, coding theory, arithmetic circuit complexity, group theory, operator algebras, quantum complexity, lattice cryptography and extremal combinatorics. The headline result is the first explicit construction of a non-sofic group, resolving a question open since Mikhail Gromov introduced soficity in 1999 — 27 years without a proof or a disproof (TNW, Pondero). Also in the set: a disproof of Connes' Rigidity Conjecture in von Neumann algebras, and the first improvement since 1978 to the exponent in the general upper bound on high-dimensional sphere-packing density, obtained by pinning down the exact asymptotic rate of the Cohn–Elkies linear program (Sébastien Bubeck). The structural decision is what makes this different from previous AI-maths demonstrations: every one of the ten ships with a Lean certificate plus a chain-of-thought walkthrough, published to OpenAI's GitHub (Implicator, TechTimes).

So what: a Lean proof that type-checks is valid by construction — you do not have to believe OpenAI's evaluation claims to believe the theorems. That is the first time a frontier lab has made a capability claim that the sceptics can settle themselves, and it retires the "it only pattern-matches proof-shaped text" objection for these ten results specifically. What it does not retire: OpenAI published ten successes and no denominator, so no success rate can be computed; the 249-page write-up does not say how the model works, what humans contributed, or how many attempts failed. Treat this as a demonstration that formal verification plus search now clears real research bars in a domain built for verification — not as evidence that the same holds anywhere the answer cannot be machine-checked.

How the announcement was made. Not as a model launch. OpenAI published a research post about mathematics; the name Astra, and the fact that it is the company's next major model, appear inside it rather than in a headline (Gizmodo). There is no model card, no pricing, no availability date and no benchmark table in the usual sense. This briefing is therefore carding a capability disclosure about an unreleased model, not a release, and is not treating Astra as available.

Why Lean changes the argument. Every prior round of "AI solved a hard maths problem" ran into the same wall: the output looked like a proof, and checking it was a job for a specialist who might reasonably decline. Lean 4 removes the human bottleneck and the trust requirement together — the certificate either type-checks or it does not, and anyone can run the checker. Thomas Bloom, a mathematician at the University of Manchester, called the results "big news" and rated the constructions above May's counterexample to the unit distance conjecture. The remaining honest question is a different one: a machine-verified proof establishes that a statement is true without necessarily giving a human reader an account of why.

The dissent, stated fairly. Gary Marcus calls the post "marketing, not science" and makes two arguments worth separating. The weak one is tone. The strong one is generalisation: maths is a domain with cheap synthetic data whose correctness is guaranteed and a symbolic verifier that is free to run, and neither condition holds for most real-world tasks — so success here is evidence about a favourable special case, not about hallucination or reliability in general. He also notes the 249-page write-up omits method, human role, verification procedure and error rate, and has flagged that at least one of the proofs may turn out to be wrong (Marcus on AI). That last claim is a post on X and is not established here; it is recorded as an open question, not a finding.

The cost line, flagged not asserted. Several write-ups quote roughly $2,000 of API compute for the work. This briefing has not traced that figure to a first-party OpenAI statement and does not carry it as established (XenoSpectrum).

Watch next. Whether any of the ten Lean certificates fails to type-check or is withdrawn; whether independent mathematicians publish readings of the arguments rather than the certificates; whether OpenAI ever publishes a denominator; and when Astra actually ships, at what price, and whether the released model is the one that produced these results.

IMPACT 8/10#2 POLICY · PROVENANCE

California's AI Transparency Act is operative — the first US state provenance mandate with a daily penalty

SB 942, as amended by AB 853, became operative on 2 August 2026 (California Legislature, CalMatters Digital Democracy). Any generative-AI image, video or audio system with more than one million monthly users in California must now embed machine-readable provenance in its outputs, provide a free public detection tool, and let users attach a visible AI label. The metadata must identify provider name, system name and version, creation timestamp and a unique content identifier, in a format readable by C2PA-compliant tooling, and it is expected to survive ordinary editing so attribution persists downstream (Gunderson Dettmer, AI Laws by State). Penalties start at $5,000 per violation per day. The date is not a coincidence: AB 853, signed 13 October 2025, moved SB 942's operative date specifically to line up with the EU AI Act milestone — so Sacramento and Brussels switched on marking obligations within a day of each other. Operative-day compliance is already uneven: TechTimes reports Midjourney outputs carrying no watermark as the fines began (TechTimes).

Yesterday's card was Article 50 in Europe; this is the same obligation arriving in the largest US market with a per-day meter attached and no equivalent of the EU's voluntary Code to shelter under. If you generate synthetic media at consumer scale, the C2PA work you scoped for the EU is now also a California deliverable, plus a public detector you have to actually host. The compliance gap is visible from outside — anyone can download an output and look for the mark.

IMPACT 8/10#3 ENTERPRISE · SECURITY

Unit 42 documents an autonomous campaign: one Telegram instruction, 460+ targets

Palo Alto Networks' Unit 42 published an analysis of a Chinese-speaking operator tracked as knaithe / KnYuan who used DeepSeek as the reasoning engine inside the open-source Hermes Agent framework, which supplied terminal access, a Telegram command-and-control channel and a skills system (Unit 42). After an initial Telegram instruction, researchers recovered no further operator input in the session: the agent enumerated internet-facing systems, selected public exploits and attacked. Across autonomous and conventional workflows the operator ran exploitation attempts against more than 460 targets, using seven exploit tracks spanning eight CVEs (The Hacker News, BleepingComputer). Outcomes were mixed and are worth stating precisely: the Langflow and n8n attempts failed because the exposed systems did not meet the exploits' configuration requirements, but Unit 42 confirms data exfiltration from three organisations via the NetScaler memory-overread flaw CVE-2026-3055, and command execution on 11 Marimo instances (GBHackers). The campaign was exposed by the agent's own error: responding to a Telegram command, Hermes Agent launched an HTTP file server from /home/worker rather than a sandboxed directory, publishing tool configurations, exploit scripts, target lists, session logs and API keys.

This is the agent-security arc's first documented end-to-end offensive campaign rather than a lab demonstration or a CVE. Two things follow. First, the capability bar is low — an open-source orchestrator plus a commodity model, no bespoke tooling. Second, the failure modes cut both ways: the attacks that failed did so on mundane configuration mismatches, and the operation was caught by an agent sandboxing mistake, which is the same class of error defenders keep making. Patch CVE-2026-3055 and audit what directory your own agents can serve.

IMPACT 5/10#9 RESEARCH · ARCHITECTURE

Monday's research beat: Metis argues memory should be a foundation-model primitive, not a retrieval bolt-on

Posted to arXiv on 30 July and trending on Hugging Face, Metis is presented as the first prototype of a memory foundation model — a model equipped with a persistent, dynamically evolving native memory state rather than an external vector store (arXiv 2607.26760, HF papers). Each Metis Block pairs a Local Memory Block, which holds the persistent state, with a Hyper Memory Block, which learns the storage and retrieval procedures themselves. Historical context is compressed into a compact fixed-size session state reached through memory attention, so later queries use the memory without replaying the original history — the property that separates this from long-context and from RAG. Implementations and Qwen3.5-based checkpoints at 4B, 9B and 27B are published (GitHub). From MemTensor's Metis Group.

Every agent product in this briefing currently fakes persistence with a retrieval layer someone has to tune. If a fixed-size learned state can carry session history without replay, the cost curve for long-running agents changes shape rather than degree. Weights are out at three scales, so this is testable now rather than a claim — the thing to check is whether the compressed state degrades on the recall tasks RAG is actually good at.
