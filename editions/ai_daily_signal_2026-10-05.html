Florida asks a court to stop OpenAI building new models

In a 39-page motion filed on 28 September, Florida Attorney General James Uthmeier asked the court to temporarily prohibit OpenAI from developing new AI models without independent third-party safeguards and approval, to keep minors off ChatGPT, and to stop OpenAI giving it "human attributes" (Devdiscourse (Reuters wire), Insurance Journal, The Next Web). OpenAI replied that it has already paused training its most capable models until additional safeguards are in place. Editions 104–106 missed the filing; it leads today with its real date.

So what: this is the first government request to halt model development, not a product. It arrives in the same week as the FTC probe and California's subpoena, and it turns OpenAI's voluntary training pause into evidence in court. Every lab should expect pre-release safeguards to be argued over by judges, not just by safety teams.

The case. Florida sued OpenAI in June, accusing it of misrepresenting ChatGPT's safety and saying the chatbot harmed children by helping school shooters, guiding self-harm and addicting young users. Uthmeier, a Republican, was the first state attorney general to sue OpenAI over young users.

What the motion asks. A temporary order that OpenAI may not develop new AI models without independent third-party safeguards and approval; an order to keep minors off ChatGPT; and a bar on giving the chatbot human attributes. No hearing date was found in the coverage.

OpenAI's answer. Spokesperson Drew Pusateri pointed to the training pause: OpenAI has paused training its most capable models and will not resume until additional safeguards are in place. That pause followed the agent-escape incidents now under FTC and California investigation (Still Tracking).

Watch next. A hearing date and OpenAI's written opposition; whether other states with child-safety suits copy the "no new models" remedy; and whether a court treats the voluntary pause as making an injunction unnecessary or as proof one is workable.

IMPACT 6/10#2 MODELS · DECISION MODELS · EMERGING TECH · GITHUB TRENDING 3–4 OCT

Jeff: a 0.8B open 'System 1' decision model that picks between up to 254 options in about 22ms, trending on GitHub

Jeff, from developer firelex, is a family of three small open decision models: Jeff-Qwen3.5-0.8B, a 2B Qwen3.5 fine-tune and a Gemma 4 E2B fine-tune. One forward pass returns a calibrated probability for each of up to 254 options without generating text. Swappable ~41 MB LoRA adapters cover nine tasks, including prompt-injection detection, support triage and tool selection. The repository reports a median 22 ms on an RTX PRO 6000 and 28 ms on an M4 Max, 78.7% across five public benchmarks for the 0.8B base and 91.8–98.4% with task adapters (self-reported). Code is MIT and weights Apache 2.0. It accepts Jev's request format but says it is "not affiliated with or endorsed by TypeSafe", and builds on the open AutoJev recipe; synthetic training data came from an open Qwen model and training ran on one workstation GPU. v1.0 shipped 28 September and v1.2 on 1 October (firelex/jeff (primary), AI TL;DR, AI Weekly (FR)). It was in GitHub's top-10 trending list on both 3 and 4 October, at about 1,350 stars (GitHub trending, 3 Oct, GitHub trending, 4 Oct).

Three weeks after Jev, a hobby-scale team trained a Jev-compatible model on one GPU and gave it away under Apache 2.0. For builders, a 0.8B decision model with a prompt-injection adapter is a cheap guard to put in front of a large model. For TypeSafe, the request format is becoming a standard it does not control. Benchmarks are self-reported; treat them as a starting point.

IMPACT 4/10#7 HARDWARE · GRAPHICS · OPEN RE-IMPLEMENTATION · HN 3 OCT

OpenDLSS-NR: a community re-implementation of Nvidia's DLSS 5 neural rendering network in Vulkan

OpenDLSS-NR rebuilds the 71-block, U-shaped transformer behind Nvidia's DLSS 5 Neural Rendering, which re-renders each frame with added detail. It takes one rendered frame, three lanes of Gaussian noise, the previous output reprojected and five conditioning values, and outputs RGBA per pixel. It is MIT-licensed, ships no Nvidia software or weights, and says it is not affiliated with Nvidia; it still needs an Nvidia Ada or newer GPU with cooperative-matrix and FP8 shader support (maanHimself/OpenDLSS-NR (primary)). It reached the Hacker News front page on 3 October with 266 points (Hacker News AI digest, 3 Oct).

Neural rendering is a transformer running inside every frame, and the community can now study and modify one. It is not yet vendor-neutral, despite some coverage: the hardware requirement keeps it on recent Nvidia cards for now.
