■ Today's Lead · New · Models · Day 1 of the gemini-4-cadence arc

Google has stopped pre-training Gemini 4 and wants it out early — the first frontier-cadence move since last week's price war

Koray Kavukcuoglu, elevated to senior vice-president of Google DeepMind on 12 August, used his first public appearance — The Information's AI Agenda Live Summit on 23–24 September — to say Gemini 4 has entered early post-training. The quote, as relayed across coverage published on 25 September: "Our intention is to roll out an early post-training version as soon as possible, because we've already seen promising results and are very excited" (Yahoo Finance, TechBriefly, Dataconomy). Reaching post-training means the base architecture is done and the work now is fine-tuning for safety, alignment and reliable real-world output. Reporting puts the start of the pre-training run at 21 July, and the model's stated focus at coding, autonomous agents and long-horizon agentic workflows rather than text generation. No release date was given; the only commitment is "much earlier than the end of the year" (Android Headlines, BigGo).

So what: the two weeks since Opus 5.5 and the GPT-6 Sol/Luna price cut have had no Google answer in them — this is the answer's timetable, not the answer. If your 2027 model-selection or pricing assumptions were built on a Gemini refresh landing in Q1, move them forward a quarter and hold the rest: an accelerated post-training schedule is exactly the kind of commitment that slips, and an "early post-training version" is a preview, not a GA model you can build a contract on.

What was actually said. One sentence, at one event, by one executive. Kavukcuoglu confirmed Gemini 4 is in early post-training and said the intention is to ship an early post-training version as soon as possible. Everything else in today's coverage — the 21 July pre-training start, the coding/agents/long-horizon focus, the "much earlier than year-end" target — is reporting around that sentence rather than further quotation. Treat the sentence as solid and the surrounding detail as well-corroborated but secondary.

Why the venue matters. This was his first public appearance since the August promotion to SVP, at a paid industry summit rather than a Google product event. That is where you put a timetable you want competitors and customers to hear without publishing a blog post you can be held to. There is no DeepMind blog entry, no model card, no benchmark, no pricing.

The competitive frame, stated carefully. Several outlets covering this put Google's current frontier offering meaningfully behind OpenAI and Anthropic on composite intelligence indices, with one placing the gap at roughly 40 percent. We could not verify that figure against a named benchmark and are not repeating it as fact. What is verifiable from this briefing's own record: Anthropic shipped Opus 5.5 at $4/$20 on 22 September and OpenAI shipped GPT-6 Sol and Luna the same day at half the flagship price, and Google has shipped nothing into that window. An accelerated Gemini 4 is a response to that fortnight whether or not any particular index gap is the right number.

What "early post-training version" buys you, and what it doesn't. Post-training is where safety tuning, alignment and instruction-following are done. Shipping an early checkpoint out of it means accepting a model that is less settled on exactly the axes enterprises test hardest — refusal behaviour, tool-use reliability, long-horizon consistency. For a model whose stated focus is autonomous agents and long-horizon workflows, that is not a trivial trade. Read an early Gemini 4 as a preview to evaluate, not a model to migrate production agents onto.

Against the other frontier signal today. New #3 has OpenAI reportedly unveiling GPT-6 Cyber at DevDay on 29 September. Two of the three US frontier labs are therefore expected to make model news inside the next four days, both aimed at the agentic surface. The third — Anthropic — shipped four days ago.

Watch next. Whether an early checkpoint appears in AI Studio or Vertex before a public launch; whether any benchmark accompanies it; whether Google prices it against the Sol/Luna cut rather than against Gemini 3; and whether the "much earlier than year-end" framing survives a month.

IMPACT 9/10#2 POLICY · US STATE LAW

Roughly 30 California AI bills hit a 30 September deadline — and California has no pocket veto, so silence enacts them

Surfaced here for the first time; the underlying session wrapped earlier in September. California's legislature passed around 30 AI-related bills this session and Governor Newsom must sign or veto each one individually by 30 September. Critically, there is no pocket veto in California: a measure left unsigned at the deadline becomes law without his signature (Kelley Drye, Legal 500). This is not one sweeping act but a stack of narrow ones, covering companion and customer-service chatbots, AI transparency and provenance data, employment and workplace surveillance, synthetic performers and digital replicas, state oversight of independent AI auditors, and who pays for a data centre's electricity hookup (Reason). Already signed: child-safety measures AB 2246, SB 1119, AB 1709, SB 867, AB 1856, and AI-auditor oversight bills SB 813 and AB 1405; an Age-Appropriate Design Code replacement and a ban on toys containing companion chatbots were both signed on 10 September. Still pending: employment bills including SB 947, restricting employers' use of automated decision systems — defined to include anything producing a score, classification or recommendation that assists or replaces human decision-making and materially affects a person (Covington Global Policy Watch), and a bill regulating attorneys' use of AI (Law360). Separately, Newsom on 18 September directed state agencies to develop their own AI safety regulations (CalMatters, KQED).

If you deploy a chatbot, an AI hiring or performance tool, synthetic likenesses, or a data centre in California, you have four days of certainty left — and the default outcome is enactment, so build your compliance read on the bills passing rather than on a veto you can't see coming.

IMPACT 7/10#4 ENTERPRISE · AGENT GOVERNANCE

Dataiku ships a cross-platform agent inventory — the first direct answer to yesterday's agent-identity gap

Announced 24 September, generally available October 2026: Agent Management, a standalone product that finds every AI agent an enterprise is running regardless of which platform built it, measures business and technical performance, and flags the ones carrying the most risk (Dataiku, Help Net Security). It connects to AWS Bedrock, Databricks Agents, Google Vertex, Microsoft Copilot Studio and Azure Foundry, Salesforce Agentforce, Snowflake Cortex and Dataiku itself, with OpenTelemetry for custom environments, and scans them into a single inventory. It resolves each agent's structure — the tools and models it actually relies on — rather than just recording that it exists. For the highest-risk agents (customer-facing, sensitive data, live transactions) it keeps a standing record of certification status, named risks and tests that re-run on a schedule, so the evidence trail exists before a manager, auditor or regulator asks. The deliberate bet is agnosticism: incumbent agent monitoring lives inside one vendor's stack and favours that vendor's agents (Forkast). The gap it targets is real and measured: per IBM's "AI in Motion" research, fewer than one in five organisations keep a complete, current inventory of their AI systems.

This is the control that was missing from yesterday's finding that only 22% of firms treat agents as identity-bearing entities — if you run agents on more than one platform, an agnostic inventory is now procurable, and "we don't know how many agents we have" stops being a defensible answer in October.

IMPACT 9/10D1 POLICY · LAB SELF-GOVERNANCE

Day 12: the standards body is described today as federally overseen — which is not what this briefing told you yesterday

WHAT CHANGED Yesterday's New #2 relayed The Information's framing: a FINRA-style body "without government oversight", rumour-flagged, with approached names and no design. Today's sourcing describes a materially different object. The proposed Frontier AI Standards Body is reported as federally overseen and industry-funded, convening a board of independent technical experts and open-source representatives, working with federal agencies and the US National Labs on national-security-relevant evaluations, and designating models above benchmark thresholds as "Frontier-class" with their developers classified as "Frontier Labs". The mechanism is voluntary at launch — labs share models for review up to 30 days before release — then transitions to mandatory once the protocol establishes credibility. And it is further along than "reported": OpenAI's chief global affairs officer Chris Lehane confirmed at a Washington briefing on 15 September that OpenAI, Anthropic and Google DeepMind had been coordinating on safety protocols for several weeks.
Two things follow. First, the objection changes shape. Yesterday's card said the risk was a self-regulator with no government in it; if the design has federal supervision and National Labs participation, the sharper question is not absence of government but capture of it — a body funded by the firms it audits, with a statutory-looking imprimatur. That is the objection White House AI czar David Sacks has already framed, and the one Forkast and Yahoo Finance put historically: FINRA-style bodies have rarely functioned as brakes. Second, the opposition is now named. Meta, xAI and NVIDIA openly opposed new government-led regulation at Dreamforce on 15 September — the same day Lehane confirmed the coordination. This is a three-lab bloc, not an industry standard, and two frontier labs plus the dominant accelerator vendor are outside it. The lineage remains public: Demis Hassabis published the FINRA-for-AI framework in July and told Axios he wanted it operational before year-end; the Cloud Security Alliance has a standing research note on the proposal and Lawfare a design critique. Still absent: a charter, a funding commitment, a confirmed chief executive, and any on-record statement from Anthropic or Google DeepMind. Tier held at critical.

Correct yesterday's read: the thing to diligence is not a regulator-free club but a would-be SRO with federal cover and 30-day pre-release review — so the question for your vendor is whether "Frontier-class" designation would become the attestation your compliance rests on, and what happens to the two frontier labs that have declined to join.

IMPACT 9/10D2 POLICY · AGENT SECURITY

Day 2: the disputed date resolves to June, and the disclosure gap gets a hard number — 84 days

WHAT CHANGED Yesterday's lead flagged an unresolved conflict — ABC and Healthcare IT News dating the Medicare portal incident to 18 June, TIME and NPR to 18 July — and declined to pick. It resolves toward June. Tech Monitor and Computer Weekly both place it in June 2026, and a precise figure now attaches to the notification failure: the first notification did not arrive until 10 September — 84 days after the incident. Delta held at carry rather than escalated — the facts sharpened, the situation did not worsen, and no enforcement step, legislative proposal or AFP referral has been announced since.

84 days and a first notification on 10 September is now the concrete benchmark your vendor contracts get measured against — write the agent-incident disclosure SLA in days, not in "promptly", and note that the model was being evaluated internally when it did this, which is the condition most of your own agent testing runs under.
