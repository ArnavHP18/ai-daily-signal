Lean experts certify Anthropic mathematician's Claude-assisted proof of the Riemann Hypothesis as correct

A day after Levent Alpöge released a 17-million-line Lean proof, the Lean and Mathlib communities say every check passed. Mathematicians now have to understand the ideas.

CRITICAL · ACT THIS WEEKOfficial sourceOpened the Lean community's joint statement and Andrew Sutherland's MIT verification page; both describe the same checks. Not checked by us: the proof itself.
WHAT CHANGED The Lean and Mathlib communities, backed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, certify (21 Nov) that Levent Alpöge's 17-million-line Lean proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct, after independent proof checkers and two statement-matching tools all passed.
Yesterday's claim has passed the formal test. In a joint statement endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, the Lean and Mathlib communities say they have full confidence that Levent Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct and "hereby certify" both the statement and the proof. The proof is a Lean library of about 17 million lines that depends only on Mathlib. Their checks: the statement of RH is the one already in Mathlib, vetted by Lean and number-theory experts; dozens of people compiled the library and got the same proof export; two independent tools confirmed the theorem proved is exactly Mathlib's RH; and the export was accepted by Lean's own kernel and several independent checkers in different languages, including con-leche and con-ron, which come with formal proofs that they cannot accept a false proof. The checks ran on an inert export, out of reach of any malicious code in the sources. MIT's Andrew Sutherland, who ran his checks with Claude Code, reports all 175,187 modules compile, the five headline theorems use only Lean's three standard axioms, and the nanoda and con-leche kernels accept the full export. The community statement covers RH; it does not mention the Generalized Riemann Hypothesis that Alpöge also claimed, and the human explanation of the new ideas is still to come.

So what: a 167-year-old problem has a machine-checked proof found with an AI model, which is a strong signal that AI plus formal verification can produce results nobody has to take on trust. It does not break today's encryption; the practical lesson for banks is about verification, not cryptography.

On Friday Levent Alpöge, a mathematician at Anthropic, said that working with an internal Claude model for about six weeks he had proved the Riemann Hypothesis, and released the proof as Lean code. Normally a claimed proof of a famous conjecture takes referees months or years. Here the proof was written in a language a computer can check, so the question became narrower: does it compile, does it prove the right statement, and can the checker be trusted?

Within a day the Lean and Mathlib communities answered all three. The statement matches the one already in Mathlib; independent people rebuilt the 17-million-line library and got the same export; two tools confirmed the statement; and several independently written checkers, two of them formally proven unable to accept a false proof, accepted it. What remains is human work: understanding why the proof works and what new mathematics it contains.

Dragos's research team, led by HD Moore, sent about two million prompts containing unique URLs and fake credentials to widely used AI services between July and November, then watched where they surfaced. #9

GnuPG patches a critical GPGME bug that a booby-trapped key can trigger

NOTABLEOfficial sourceSecurity · patches · 20-21 Nov
The GnuPG project released GPGME 2.3.1 on Friday to fix CVE-2026-129975, which it calls a critical bug in all earlier versions. An OpenPGP key with a crafted, very long user ID triggers a double free in two routines that read output from gpg; the project says this can be used to exfiltrate data from applications, for example by sending signed data, and that remote code execution can't be ruled out. GPGME is the library most mail clients, many XMPP clients, Samba and GnuPG's own Gpg4win and GnuPG Desktop use to talk to GnuPG; new versions of those were released too. Johan Carlsson found it and GitLab reported it on 12 November; disclosure moved up from 25 November because GitLab had to ship early for the Ruby2Shell bug. No attacks are reported. Separately, pwn.ai published Upload2Shell (CVE-2026-125396), a Ghostscript sandbox escape reachable through document and image uploads, fixed upstream in Ghostscript 10.08.0 in September.

So what: banks use GnuPG for file transfers with counterparties and regulators; patch GPGME and Gpg4win on transfer servers and desktops now.
