■ Today's Lead · New · Policy · Day 1 of the open-weight-policy arc

Twenty-five companies tell Washington not to lock down open weights — and the three frontier labs did not sign

On Friday Jul 24, Nvidia and 24 other companies published a three-page letter, "Open Weights and American AI Leadership," asking the US government to avoid "premature restrictions on downloadable AI models." Jensen Huang carried it in his first post ever on X — "Don't lock down open artificial intelligence models" — writing that open models "strengthen safety and cybersecurity, accelerate innovation and diffusion, and enable sovereignty" (CNBC, Fortune). The signatory list is the story: Microsoft, Meta, IBM, Dell, Palantir, Hugging Face, CrowdStrike, Box, ServiceNow, Replit, Perplexity, Telnyx, Andreessen Horowitz, Y Combinator and Emergence Capital — chipmakers, server vendors, security firms and the venture funds that back the companies building on downloadable models. OpenAI, Anthropic and Google are absent (Tom's Hardware). It lands four days after Axios reported that parts of the administration are again exploring ways to restrict access to advanced Chinese open-weight models after Moonshot's Kimi K3 — and two days before K3's own weights drop.

So what: the open-vs-closed fight has stopped being a licensing preference and become a lobbying position with named sides — if your stack has an open-weight fallback, the thing that could remove it is now a US policy decision, not a vendor one. Write down which of your models are downloadable and where they were trained.

What was published. A three-page letter titled "Open Weights and American AI Leadership," co-signed by 25 companies, asking Washington not to impose broad restrictions on downloadable model weights. Huang posted it as his debut message on X on Jul 24 (Benzinga, Fortune). The argument reported across outlets: transparent weights let outsiders test and find flaws, closed systems concentrate power, and over-restriction pushes builders toward foreign models rather than away from them.

Why the absences matter. The three US labs with the most valuable closed frontier models — OpenAI, Anthropic, Google — did not sign (Tom's Hardware). The coalition that did sign is the layer that sells into open weights: silicon, servers, security, dev tools and the funds backing startups built on them. That is a commercial split, not a philosophical one, and it is now on the record in front of Congress.

The policy it is aimed at. Axios reported on Jul 20 that parts of the administration have explored restricting access to advanced Chinese open-source models after Kimi K3 entered the US debate; reporting also indicates a blanket prohibition was not seriously considered inside White House discussions. Separately, Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent said on Jul 21 the federal government will examine Chinese open-source models for intellectual-property theft, with possible sanctions on Chinese AI companies found to have taken IP from US firms. Roughly 200 Silicon Valley startups sent their own letter urging the White House not to block access, arguing a ban would hit small US companies while leaving the weights downloadable anyway (TechCrunch, Axios).

The caveats. The letter is advocacy, not regulation — no rule, order or bill has been published. The administration's internal position is reported, not stated: "exploring options" and "not seriously considered" are both sourced to reporting rather than to a document. The Bessent IP-review line comes from a single readout in this scan and is chip-flagged accordingly inside the reg-clock thread. Nothing here changes the legal status of any model today.

Watch next. Whether Kimi K3's Jul 27 weight drop (Developing D1, one day out) produces a concrete regulatory response; whether any of the three absent labs states a position; and whether the "premature restrictions" language shows up in the US covered-frontier framework due Aug 1.

poolside ships Laguna S 2.1 — a Western open-weight coder that fits on one DGX Spark

poolside released Laguna S 2.1 on Jul 21: an open-weight Mixture-of-Experts coding model, 118B total / 8B activated per token, context up to 1M tokens, with thinking and no-thinking modes switchable per request (poolside, GlobeNewswire). poolside bills it as the West's most capable open-weight model; on Terminal-Bench 2.1 and SWE-Bench Pro it is reported to match or beat models several times its size including DeepSeek-V4-Flash, Nvidia's Nemotron 3 Ultra and Thinking Machines' Inkling (MarkTechPost). IMPACT 4/10#10 POLICY · COPYRIGHT

The AI-music fair-use ruling everyone expected this summer just slipped to 2027

A widely-trailed "summer 2026 ruling" in UMG/Sony v. Suno — billed as the first federal decision on whether training generative AI on copyrighted recordings is fair use — is not coming. A Jun 30 amended scheduling order runs fact discovery to Sep 30 2026 and sets dispositive motions for Apr 9 2027, which puts a merits decision in 2027 at the earliest (Music Business Worldwide, Chartlex). Live in the meantime: a fight over adding 61,026 recordings identified via Audible Magic fingerprinting to the complaint, and Hagens Berman — the firm behind the $260B tobacco settlement — joining independent artists suing Suno and Udio over alleged stream-ripping of training data (TechTimes).

If your licensing plan was "wait for the July ruling," you have just been handed nine more months of legal ambiguity — price training-data risk on your own assumptions, not on a court date.

IMPACT 7/10D1 MODELS · OPEN-WEIGHT

Kimi K3's weights land tomorrow — and self-hosting is the part nobody priced

WHAT CHANGED Escalated from 2 days to 1: Moonshot publishes K3's full weights under a Modified MIT licence on Jul 27. Reported self-host arithmetic is now public — a weight-only 4-bit floor near 1.4TB (a ~594GB native MXFP4 safetensors download), 8×H100 80GB minimum just to load, against API pricing of $3/$15 per million ($0.30 cached input). And the table filled out on both sides: Qwen 3.8 at 2.4T (New #4) and poolside's Laguna S 2.1 (New #5).
K3 is 2.8T sparse MoE with a 1M context, ~50B parameters firing per token — cheap per token once resident, expensive to keep resident (TECHi, Simon Willison). Until tomorrow it stays "open-weight pending": a hosted API preview whose benchmark claims nobody outside Moonshot can reproduce. The open table below it remains largely Chinese — GLM-5.2 leading agentic coding (91.2% GPQA Diamond, 62.1% SWE-Bench Pro), with DeepSeek-V4-Pro-Max, MiniMax M3 and Kimi K2.6 clustered around 80% on SWE-Bench — but now has two fresh entries this week, one Chinese and much larger, one Western and much smaller.

Tomorrow is the first day anyone can check Moonshot's numbers. Budget the 1.4TB before you budget the benchmark: for most teams the honest answer is a hosted endpoint with an exit plan, not a self-host.

IMPACT 7/10D2 POLICY · US / EU

Six days to the US framework, seven to EU enforcement — and a third front opened on open weights

WHAT CHANGED Escalated: the calendar tightened to Aug 1 (6 days) and Aug 2 (7 days), and a new front appeared beside them — Axios reported Jul 20 that parts of the administration are exploring restrictions on Chinese open-weight models, Treasury Secretary Bessent said Jul 21 the government will examine Chinese open-source models for IP theft with possible sanctions, and 25 companies plus ~200 startups wrote back within days (Hero).
Aug 1: under the Jun 2 EO, Treasury, NSA and CISA with ONCD, OSTP and NIST must stand up a classified cyber-benchmarking process designating "covered frontier models," plus a voluntary framework offering government up to 30 days' pre-release access. Aug 2: EU AI Act Article 50 transparency and GPAI enforcement switch on — the Commission and AI Office can demand documentation, run technical evaluations, restrict or withdraw a model from the EU market and fine to EUR 15M or 3% of global turnover (artificialintelligenceact.eu, Covington). The one-year GPAI adjustment period that began Aug 2 2025 ends there.

Three separate regimes now bear on the same model inventory inside eight days. The EU one has published fines and a live legal basis; the US ones are still documents nobody outside government has read — plan against the first, monitor the other two.
