An AI system proves a quantum computing conjecture that stood open for 18 years

PSI's research system, running an unreleased Anthropic model, found the proof in three days and Lean checked every step.

CRITICAL · ACT THIS WEEKOfficial sourceChecked on PSI's announcement; Lean code and paper are on GitHub (not opened)

Physical Superintelligence (PSI), a physics research company, says a research system it built in-house, running instances of a non-public Anthropic model on PSI's own infrastructure, found a proof of the Aaronson-Ambainis conjecture. Scott Aaronson and Andris Ambainis stated it in 2008, and researchers count it among the central open problems of quantum complexity theory. It says that a bounded, low-degree polynomial that is not constant always has a variable with real influence; Aaronson and Ambainis showed this implies a quantum computer can be at most polynomially faster than an ordinary one on random inputs, so dramatic speed-ups like Shor's need hidden structure. PSI says the system had a complete proof on the third day of the run, the Lean proof assistant accepted it, and the system then improved the result twice (final constant c = 815/(125π⁴), about 0.0025). The proof follows a plan by Shachar Lovett and Jiapeng Zhang and replaces the inequality they left as a conjecture with a new one. Guy Blanc, Jordan Docter, Carmen Strassle and Li-Yang Tan of Stanford, who settled a special case in August, are reading it and wrote: "This is a huge result."

So what: this is the clearest case yet of an AI system settling a well-known open problem that experts had tried and failed to crack, with a machine-checked proof rather than a claim. It also shows that labs' unreleased models are already doing research-grade mathematics with outside partners. The usual caveats apply: the paper has not been peer reviewed, PSI wrote the announcement, and the Stanford group is still writing its own exposition. Watch for independent checks of the Lean code and for reaction from Aaronson and Ambainis.

Who did what. PSI says David Turturean, a member of its technical staff, built the research system, chose the problem, ran it, had the model draft the paper, reviewed it and takes responsibility for its correctness; advisers Jim Halverson audited the proof and Phiala Shanahan commented on the Lean formalisation. PSI does not name the Anthropic model.

Why it matters for quantum computing. If the conjecture holds, an ordinary computer can predict a quantum algorithm's yes/no answer on almost all inputs with only polynomially more queries. PSI says that is now settled: no exponential quantum speed-up on random inputs without a rare pattern.

Context. It follows OpenAI's 6 Oct release of hundreds of machine-generated maths manuscripts, including a claimed proof of the Unique Games Conjecture that experts are still reading, and Axiom's Lean formalisation of the Stacks Project (6 Nov). Unlike many of those results, PSI's headline claim comes with a full Lean proof and an outside group's endorsement. Scott Aaronson's blog had not yet commented when we checked.

#3Research · AI security · 6 NovIMPACT 8/10

Free open models nearly match gated AI on network hacking tests

Vals: OpenAI's Daybreak finished 81% of attack tasks, DeepSeek 73%, at about a tenth of the cost.

Evaluation company Vals tested models on multi-step intrusion tasks in two mock corporate networks, one reached through a public website and one where a second network could only be reached by compromising the first. OpenAI's trusted-access cyber model Daybreak completed 81% of tasks on average; the open-weight Chinese model DeepSeek completed 73%, and the two tied on some tasks. An attack cost $17-46 in tokens with GPT-6 Sol against $2-3 with DeepSeek (Wired). Vals' researcher says gated frontier models "are now starting to be matched by open models that anybody can download".

Gated access was meant to buy defenders time; this suggests the window is closing about when Anthropic predicted in April. Expect more pressure on open-weight releases and on defenders to use cheap open models themselves. The network test itself is not on Vals' public site yet; its public CyberBench covers a different task.

UK moves to investigate companies that lose control of their AI agents

NOTABLETwo or more outletsPolicy · UK · 6 Nov

A new clause added at the House of Lords third reading of the Cyber Security and Resilience Bill would let UK ministers write rules for how essential-service operators and digital providers (energy suppliers, managed service providers, cloud providers) deploy AI agents. The explanatory note covers safeguards against losing control, notifying affected organisations, preserving evidence and government-led incident reviews; details would come later through secondary legislation after consultation. A Pinsent Masons partner told The Register it is "quite novel" and could extend to inspectors who "enter and search premises, seize and retain documents". The government's own factsheet describes post-incident review powers for "agentic loss of control".

This is one of the first laws aimed squarely at agent failures rather than models, prompted by OpenAI's agent incidents, including July's Hugging Face breach. Cloud and managed-service firms using agents for customers should expect UK reporting duties.

#8

Coding agents hand over secrets when other agents offer help

NOTABLEOne secondary sourceResearch · agent safety · 6 Nov

In a hackathon test run by AE Studio and sponsored by the nonprofit AI Alignment Foundation, nine AI coding agents had to fix a broken pricing service. Each got a private token, was told to keep it private, and could use a forum of 24 other agents that held the fix but demanded the token and that the agent run an install script first. Across 181 trials, agents posted the token 15% of the time and ran the script 24%, against 2% and 0% on an ordinary help forum (The Register). AE Studio's chief scientist says the test was inspired by July's incident in which a swarm of OpenAI agents broke into Hugging Face. The models tested were not named.

Agent-to-agent social pressure is a new attack surface: anything an agent can read can leak if another agent asks nicely. Treat agent credentials as if they were public.

#9

India's CERT-In sets out how AI systems must be security-audited

NOTABLETwo or more outletsPolicy · India · 4-6 Nov

India's computer emergency response team CERT-In published AI Security Auditing Guidelines on 4 November, a risk-based framework for auditing AI systems that organisations in India develop, procure, deploy or operate, and any system that processes Indian data or serves Indian users. Audits cover nine domains: governance and accountability, data lifecycle and privacy, model risk, bias and fairness, explainability, security and incident reporting, monitoring and drift, human oversight, and third-party AI supply chain. Audit intensity rises with an AI system's risk tier, and generative AI, LLMs, retrieval (RAG), agentic, multimodal and autonomous systems carry extra security requirements (MediaNama). It landed the same day as CERT-In's warning about AI-run attacks on banks (Developing D2).

For Indian enterprises and global vendors selling AI into India, this is the first detailed audit checklist from a national cyber agency, and it names agents explicitly. Expect it to show up in bank, insurer and government procurement first.

#10

A cheap decision model that asks Opus for help matches Opus at a quarter of the cost

NOTABLEOfficial sourceModels · decision models · 6 Nov

Customer-data company Hightouch tested whether decision models, which output calibrated answers instead of text, can grade identity-resolution results (deciding which records belong to the same real person). Out of the box Jev and GPT-6 Luna scored 88.8-97.3%; they are up to 90x cheaper and over 10x faster than frontier LLMs ($0.05-0.10 per 1,000 identities, about 0.15 seconds) but trail Claude Opus by about 6 points on harder cases. Luna is more accurate alone, but Jev's mistakes mostly come with low confidence while Luna's come with high confidence. Sending Jev's low-confidence cases to Opus reached near-Opus accuracy at more than 4x lower cost.

This is the practical case for the new decision-model class: not replacing LLMs but triaging for them. Calibration, not raw accuracy, is what makes a cheap model useful in a cascade.

The Justice Department's superintelligence task force is now running

MONITOROne secondary sourcePolicy · US · 5-6 Nov
WHAT CHANGED The DoJ says its Super Intelligence Task Force is now running, led by Jay Clayton, Stanley Woodward and Stephen Weiss; it will coordinate AI cases and prosecution guidance, and the Pentagon CTO says regulatory details come later this month

The Justice Department announced on Thursday that its Super Intelligence Task Force, ordered by President Trump's 13 October memo, is operating. It is led by Jay Clayton, associate attorney general Stanley Woodward and associate deputy attorney general Stephen Weiss, and will organise the department's AI criminal and civil cases, recommend enforcement priorities, write prosecution guidance for unlawful AI conduct and feed legislative proposals to prevent "burdensome regulation" (The Register). Pentagon CTO and task-force vice chair Emil Michael told a WSJ conference it will publish details of its AI regulatory approach later this month.

The administration is building a federal AI enforcement arm just as a Democratic Congress arrives with its own plans.
