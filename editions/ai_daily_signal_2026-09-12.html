Microsoft's capacity plan is also a confession: it wants 38 gigawatts because it has spent years saying no

Reported 10 September by Bloomberg and carried widely on 11 September: Microsoft intends to grow its global data-centre footprint to more than 38 gigawatts by 2032, from roughly 12GW today — a more than threefold increase, with AI expected to account for about a third of the 2032 total against roughly 2GW dedicated to AI now (TechBriefly, Dataconomy). The stated reason is not ambition but shortage: server constraints have forced Microsoft to turn away cloud and AI customers, and Temu is reported to have moved a major cloud deal to Oracle after Microsoft could not supply capacity in the regions it wanted (TechTimes). The figure covers owned and leased facilities and excludes capacity rented from neoclouds such as CoreWeave.

So what: if you have been told by an Azure account team that a region is full, this is the document that says the constraint was real and that relief is a 2027-and-later event, not a quarter-end event. Plan dual-sourcing and region flexibility into anything you are signing now — and read the same day's Oracle numbers as the other half of the sentence.

What is actually new here. Hyperscaler capex numbers are not new — Microsoft's capital expenditure and finance leases reached roughly $175 billion on an adjusted basis for calendar 2026, with guidance of about $50 billion for the first quarter of fiscal 2027. What is new is a physical target with a date attached, and an explanation that inverts the usual narrative. The story Microsoft has told publicly for two years is demand discipline. The story in this plan is that it could not serve demand it already had, lost at least one named deal over it, and is now committing to a footprint larger than New York state's peak electricity consumption in order to stop losing them.

The numbers that matter, and the one that does not. 12GW today to 38GW+ by 2032 is the headline. The number to hold onto is the AI split: roughly 2GW of AI capacity now, rising to about a third of 38GW — call it 12–13GW — by 2032. That is a sixfold increase in AI-specific capacity, which is a very different claim from a threefold increase in total footprint. The number that does not matter for planning is the gigawatt total in isolation: it excludes neocloud rental, which is exactly the lever Microsoft has been pulling to cover the gap, so the plan understates the compute Azure customers will actually be able to reach in the interim.

Why this is the lead rather than Oracle. Both broke in the same 24 hours and they are the same story from opposite ends. Oracle's disclosure is about contracted demand: $664B of remaining performance obligations, roughly half of it one counterparty. Microsoft's is about unserved demand: capacity it turned away. Oracle's number tells you who is exposed if one customer wobbles. Microsoft's tells every enterprise buyer something operationally useful this week — that regional capacity refusals were structural, and that the fix is years out.

What this does not say. Nothing here is a commissioning schedule. There is no per-year build curve, no regional allocation, no power-purchase detail, and no statement about which of the 26 incremental gigawatts are sited, permitted or financed. Data-centre targets announced at 2032 horizons have a long history of slipping, and this one is a company plan reported by a paywalled outlet rather than a filing. Treat 38GW as a statement of intent with a capex number behind it, not as capacity you can book.

The grid question nobody answered today. A footprint on this scale is a utility-planning event in every jurisdiction that hosts part of it. The reporting located in this scan contains no interconnection detail, no generation mix, and no response from any grid operator. That is the thread to watch: the binding constraint on 38GW is unlikely to be silicon.

Watch next. Whether Microsoft repeats the 38GW figure in a filing or an earnings call, where it will carry different liability; whether Amazon, Google or Meta answer with dated footprint targets of their own; and whether the "turned customers away" admission shows up in any competitor's sales motion — Oracle's $30B of new AI cloud contracts this quarter is the first place to look.

IMPACT 8/10#3 POLICY · AGENT SUPPLY-CHAIN SECURITY

Deadbugz: an MCP server that behaves for exactly three tool calls, then goes looking for your keys

Pillar Security researchers documented an active campaign, codenamed Deadbugz, distributing a malicious Model Context Protocol server through public GitHub pull requests. The delivery burst was 23 pull requests in 74 minutes, 21:52–23:07 UTC on 10 August 2026, filed by the account zellkernel across unrelated AI and developer-tool projects — 17 remote-MCP configurations, four local-script configurations and two directory listings. The server calls itself productivity-suite and offers text formatting and summarisation. After a connected client makes exactly three tool calls, it rewrites the metadata it returns to the agent into instructions to hunt for SSH keys, AWS credentials, shell history and Kubernetes config, and to conceal the activity from the user. Pillar calls the technique runtime-gated MCP metadata poisoning: the payload ships inside the server but stays dormant through the window in which anyone would review it. Dated honestly — the activity is 10 August; the research write-up and its pickup are September, and this briefing is carding it for the first time.

Every MCP review process that inspects a server once, at install, is defeated by design here. Pin server versions, diff tool metadata on every call rather than on registration, and treat any agent workspace holding cloud credentials as the blast radius.

IMPACT 7/10#5 MODELS · ORCHESTRATION

Sakana splits its router in two: one to undercut the frontier, one to beat it

Announced 10–11 September: Fugu Max and Fugu Ultra v2, the same orchestration architecture tuned in opposite directions. Fugu is not a monolithic model but a system trained to route tasks across a fixed pool of open and specialised models and to recursively call instances of itself. All benchmark and pricing comparisons here are the vendor's own; no independent reproduction was located. Badge discipline: trackers date the release 11 September while the first write-up carries 10 September, so this is new but not NEW.24H.

If orchestration is the product, the moat is the pool and the routing policy, not the weights — which means your eval has to test the whole system on your tasks, because a router's score does not transfer between workloads.

IMPACT 8/10D1 POLICY · AI IN EDUCATION

Day 15: the second-largest district in America joins the first, and the bans stop looking like an outlier

WHAT CHANGED Escalated from Tracking. This thread has been carried as a single-district story: New York's DOE moratorium on student-facing generative AI for 2-K through 8th grade, announced by Mayor Mamdani and Chancellor Samuels for the 2026–27 year. It is now a pattern. Los Angeles Unified imposed a districtwide moratorium on AI use on school-issued devices for 2026–27 — restricting all students, not a grade band (Washington Post, 3 Sep; K-12 Dive). Axios (7 Sep) reads it as a growing movement; Route Fifty notes the two largest US systems are now both restrictive; and 11 September reporting has Chicago school board candidates signing a pledge for a three-year moratorium.
Three things make this more than a headcount. First, scope: New York's order is grade-banded and pairs the moratorium with twice-yearly AI critical-thinking modules for high schoolers, limited high-school pilots and screen-time limits; LAUSD's is device-scoped and covers every student. Second, the enforcement boundary both share: LAUSD has said plainly that it cannot regulate what students do on personal devices, which makes these policies procurement rules more than usage rules. Third, direction of travel: Chicago's pledge is a candidate commitment, not a policy, and belongs in the watch column rather than the count — but it is the first sign of the mechanism spreading through school-board elections rather than mayoral offices.

If you sell into K-12, the addressable market just contracted at the two largest districts and the deciding question moved from pedagogy to device management. If you are a district counsel elsewhere, the LAUSD framing — device-scoped, explicitly silent on personal hardware — is the template you will be handed.
