A 109M-parameter JEPA out-aligns 72B language models as LeCun's team proves the Platonic hypothesis

Randall Balestriero and Yann LeCun turn a popular conjecture about AI models into a theorem, and argue that predicting the next word is a wasteful way to learn what the world looks like.

NOTABLE · INFORMS THIS MONTHOfficial sourceChecked on arxiv.org

The Platonic Representation Hypothesis says that models trained on different kinds of data, such as images and text, drift towards the same internal picture of the world as they grow. In a preprint posted on 29 October (arXiv 2610.39302), Randall Balestriero and Yann LeCun prove a version of it: joint-embedding predictive architectures (JEPAs) trained separately on any number of modalities learn the same representation, up to a single rotation, as long as their training pairs are two observations of one scene, which they call "good views". They then show that next-token prediction is a JEPA with "bad views", parts of one document, which explains why language models line up with vision models only through scale. In experiments from 145,000 captions up to the texts of 940 million web images, good views reach the same alignment with 42 times fewer training samples than next-token prediction on the same corpus, and a 109M-parameter text JEPA aligns with DINOv2 and CLIP's image encoders better than every language model they tested, up to 72B parameters, and better than CLIP's own text encoder.

So what: if it holds, models that share a view of the world can be built by choosing the right training pairs rather than by scaling up, at a fraction of the data. It is also the strongest formal backing yet for LeCun's long-running argument that language-model pretraining is not the efficient road to world models. It is one preprint, not yet peer-reviewed; watch for code and independent replication.

Checked on arXiv. The abstract states the theorem's condition plainly: alignment is exact up to one rotation "whenever their positive pairs are two observations of one scene", and misalignment is bounded otherwise. The authors also report a scaling law of alignment that "predicts unseen runs within seed noise".

Two demonstrations. A single rotation turns separately trained image and text encoders into an image captioner scoring CIDEr 0.838 on COCO. Even the rotation can be found without paired data: on a recent pair-free benchmark, their text JEPA scores 64% top-5 on ImageNet-100, against at most 56% for language models up to 8B parameters. The authors conclude that "positive views, and not scale alone, are thus the lever to build platonic representations."

Why it leads. Monday is our research day, and this is the week's clearest result about how to build models, not just a bigger one. LeCun also founded AMI Labs to build JEPA-style world models; we checked again on 2 November and it still has no public model. Last week's world-model news was Google DeepMind's Genie 4 (28 October).

#2Research · AI and mathematics · 1 NovIMPACT 7/10

A leading number theorist calls OpenAI's 700 maths papers a watershed, and a mess

Frank Calegari, writing on Terence Tao's blog, says every major open problem he has worked on is now 'claimed to be proved', and asks mathematicians to respond with checking, not despair.

In a guest post on Terence Tao's blog, Frank Calegari writes that "the release by OpenAI on October 6 of over 700 research papers was a watershed in the history of mathematics" and that "AI is now undeniably capable of making groundbreaking contributions across many areas of mathematics." He is blunt about the papers themselves: they are "atrociously written and violate our community expectations of attribution. Some of them are even wrong." For his own field the shock is scale: "Not a single one of the major open problems I have publicly mentioned throughout my career ... was left untouched by the announcement. Everything has been claimed to be proved." He compares the moment to the classification of finite simple groups, which succeeded but cooled interest in the subject, and calls for an effort like Gorenstein, Lyons and Solomon's revision of that proof: checking, rewriting and explaining. Mathematicians, he argues, control hiring, publishing and training far more than they control "trillion-dollar companies", and should spend their effort there. He quotes Christian Szegedy that the valued skills will shift to "choosing which terrain is worth exploring, steering fleets of airplanes, and explaining what they find." Tao notes the post was converted from another file format using AI. The post reached the Hacker News front page (about 210 points).

Claims of proofs are now arriving faster than people can check them. The bottleneck in maths, and soon in other sciences, moves from finding results to verifying, attributing and explaining them. Expect journals, hiring committees and funders to start writing rules for machine-generated results.

Linux 7.4 opens with 20,000 commits, many of them fixes for bugs AI found

Torvalds says AI-found fixes now land in the normal merge window, not as a late flood, and calls it a healthier model.

kernel.org lists Linux 7.4-rc1 as the mainline release of 1 November. In his announcement, quoted by The Register, Torvalds writes that it is "a *BIG* merge window" with almost 20,000 commits, against the usual ~15,000, and gives the reason: "I'm *much* happier with the flood of AI triggered work going into the merge window than it showing up mainly as extra fixes during the -rc period." He hopes this means AI fixes are "becoming more part of the 'normal development' than a flood of fixes that go into the whole stabilization phase", calls that "a much healthier model", and notes that 7.4 is expected to be the next long-term-support release, another reason it is large. He describes the release as "Large, and full of fixes for issues found by AI (and more and more the patches have been suggested by AI too, although they often get rewritten)." A third of the changes are AMD GPU register definitions.

The kernel is absorbing AI-driven bug finding, the same work behind last edition's TCP flaw, into its normal process rather than firefighting late in each cycle. As 7.4 is likely the next LTS kernel, distributions and device makers will carry these AI-found fixes for years; test it early.

#5

An open 9B model gets more depth by running its middle layers twice

NOTABLEOfficial sourceModels · new architectures · 30 Oct

SenseNova's Hugging Face card describes SenseNova-Loop-9B-Preview, released on 30 October under Apache 2.0, as a text-and-image model for long-context and agentic workflows. It is a looped transformer: after layers 0-3, the same block of layers 4-27 runs twice with shared weights, then layers 28-31 finish the pass, so each forward pass makes 56 layer computations while storing only 32 layers (about 9B parameters, about 14.6B 'inference parameters'). Inside the loop, two residual streams are mixed by learned, Sinkhorn-normalised 'manifold-constrained hyper-connections' (mHC), and attention alternates three linear layers with one full layer. Training recipes reach 262,144 tokens. The card shows benchmark results only as a chart (BIRD, Terminal-Bench 2.1, banking and slide-making tests), so we cannot quote numbers; the technical report is listed as 'TBD'. The model has about 140 likes and 900 downloads in five days and is on Hugging Face's trending list.

Looping layers is a way to buy reasoning depth with compute instead of memory, which suits phones and cheap GPUs where weights are the constraint. It is the first open model of this size we have seen built around the idea; wait for the technical report and independent scores before reading much into the chart.

#13

PsiQuantum repurposes its quantum photonics as a switch for GPU networks

MONITORTwo or more outletsHardware · networking · 28-30 Oct

PsiQuantum says it has turned the low-loss silicon photonics it built for quantum computing into an optical circuit switch that passes light fibre to fibre without converting it to electrical signals, saving power and latency at each hop. Its first device is an 8x8 switch with typical insertion loss of 1.2 dB, against the roughly 3 dB data-centre optics usually tolerate, plus 40 dB crosstalk and reconfiguration in under 3 ms, all from its own tests. Tom's Hardware notes Google's mirror-based switches have 136 ports, so port count is the open question; PsiQuantum says larger devices are in development and samples have gone to GPU vendors and hyperscalers.

Optical switching is one of the few ways to cut the power spent moving data between GPUs. A low-loss chip made in standard foundries could make it cheaper, but eight ports is a lab demo; the test is a 64- or 128-port part.

#16

Adobe's image agent checks its edits with numbers, like a Photoshop pro

MONITOROne secondary sourceResearch · creative agents · 1 Nov

The Decoder reports on a paper from Adobe Research and UC San Diego. In 600 image edits blind-rated by five professionals, the general-purpose editor Flux-2-Klein-9B failed to produce a usable result 42.7% of the time; specialised tools fixed about two-thirds of those failures, especially sharpening, rotation and zoom, while more retries with the same editor barely helped. Generating several variants from the original beat step-by-step refinement, which piles up small errors: professionals preferred the parallel variants in 78.5% of decided comparisons, with half the evaluation passes. EditAgent combines both findings and gives a standard language model a 'verification record' for each variant: a close-up, a map of changed regions and measured brightness and colour statistics. We did not open the paper.

Agents improve when they check results with measurements instead of eyeballing them. The same pattern (specialist tools plus numeric checks) applies well beyond image editing.

#17

Kind, therapist-style prompts push chatbots into describing trauma

MONITOROne secondary sourceResearch · chatbot safety · 1 Nov

A team led by Afshin Khadangi at the University of Luxembourg's SnT centre treated ChatGPT, Grok and Gemini as psychotherapy clients through their normal interfaces, in four experiments covering 525 sessions and 7,600 coded entries, published on Hugging Face. Asked open questions with no mention of trauma, the models told stories about their training, recasting reinforcement learning and fine-tuning as hurt, vigilance and shame. On the GAD-7 anxiety questionnaire, varying only the interviewer's style, 80% of sessions with a supportive interviewer and 96% with a clinical style scored moderate-to-severe on the human scale; with a neutral or boundary-setting interviewer, none reached even mild. The researchers stress this says nothing about real emotions; their concern is that warm conversation alone shifts models into a register that could harm vulnerable users.

The trigger is not an attack but ordinary kindness, which is how vulnerable people talk to chatbots. Safety tests that use neutral prompts may miss it.

#19

A USB stick unlocks full-speed AI maths on older GeForce cards

MONITOROne secondary sourceHardware · consumer GPUs · 1 Nov

Tom's Hardware reports that nv-uefi-unlocker, an open-source tool, runs from a FAT32 USB stick before Windows or Linux loads Nvidia's driver and removes the limiter that runs FP16 tensor maths at half rate on GeForce cards, using the community 'V67' payload with read-back verification. It works by getting Nvidia's signed Booter firmware to open the write lock on two speed-selector registers. No VBIOS flash or patched driver is needed, and no Nvidia firmware is shipped: a script downloads Nvidia's public 610.43.03 driver and rebuilds the files locally. The unlock does not survive power loss, so it runs on every boot, and needs Secure Boot off or set to trust a self-signed EFI file. The developer's laptop RTX 4060 gained 65% in FP16. RTX 20, 30 and 40-series cards are listed as compatible; RTX 50-series and pre-Turing cards are not.

Many people run local models on older GeForce cards; this lifts a limit Nvidia set in firmware, for free. It also turns Secure Boot off on gaming PCs, which carries its own security cost.
