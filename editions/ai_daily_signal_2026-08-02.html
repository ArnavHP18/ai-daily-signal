Brussels stops counting and starts enforcing

As of this morning the European Commission's AI Office, working with national authorities, begins enforcing the AI Act against providers of general-purpose AI models — and the transparency rules in Article 50 start to apply to any AI system placed on the EU market (European Commission, Shaping Europe's digital future). The obligations themselves are not new; they have applied since 2 August 2025. What changes today is that the Commission can act on them: demand technical documentation under Article 91, require risk-mitigation measures under Article 93, compel model access to run its own evaluations under Article 92, restrict or withdraw a model from the EU market, and fine up to €15M or 3% of global turnover, whichever is higher, under Article 101 (EU AI Act — Chapter V enforcement). Refusing or stalling on any of those requests is itself a finable offence. Article 50 lands on everyone else: chatbots must disclose they are machines, deepfakes must be labelled, and AI-generated or altered content must carry machine-readable marks so it can be detected downstream (Identity Week, Xinhua). Meanwhile the other half of this arc went quiet: EO 14409's 1 August deliverable — the classified benchmarking process for designating a "covered frontier model" — passed yesterday with no publication of the framework visible in open sources at the time of this edition.

So what: the asymmetry that this briefing has been counting down to for 54 editions has now resolved in one direction. Europe has a supervisor with subpoena-and-fine powers and a labelling rule that applies to every consumer surface you ship into the EU; Washington's deadline produced a government artefact that nobody outside government can currently read. If you ship into the EU, the actionable item this week is Article 50 — disclosure on conversational surfaces and machine-readable marking on generated media — not the GPAI documentation, which your model provider owes.

What actually switched on. Two distinct things, and conflating them is the most common error in the compliance commentary. First, enforcement: the AI Office's supervisory powers over GPAI providers under Chapter V become exercisable. Providers were already required to document their models, adopt a copyright policy and publish a sufficiently detailed summary of training content — those duties date from August 2025 — but until today the Commission had no penalty instrument behind them. Second, transparency: Article 50's user-facing obligations begin to apply to AI systems on the EU market, covering machine disclosure in interactive systems, deepfake labelling, and machine-readable marking of synthetic media, plus notification for emotion-recognition and biometric-categorisation systems.

What it is not. Today is not the high-risk (Annex III) compliance date for every obligation in the Act, and it is not a new obligation on GPAI providers. The Council gave final approval in June 2026 to a simplification package that moved some deadlines (Council of the EU); this briefing is not asserting that every downstream date survived unchanged. What is firmly established is the enforcement-and-Article-50 pairing the Commission itself announced.

The industry's pre-emptive move. Roughly 190 organisations had signed the Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content by the end of July — the voluntary instrument that gives a presumption of Article 50 conformity (New #2 below). The provider list includes Anthropic, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Mistral, OpenAI, Cohere, Aleph Alpha, Black Forest Labs and Synthesia; the deployer list runs to Getty Images, Lenovo, Lufthansa, Bulgari, Iberdrola and Fastweb. Roughly half the signatories are small or recently founded companies (European Commission).

The Washington side. EO 14409, signed 2 June, gave Treasury, NSA and CISA sixty days to produce a classified benchmarking process for "covered frontier model" designation and a voluntary framework for up to 30 days of pre-release government access (White House, Norton Rose Fulbright). A draft circulated to OpenAI, Anthropic and Google in mid-July, with NSA and Commerce's CAISI named as reviewers and the three firms returning edits (DigiTimes). An announcement was widely expected on or before 1 August. This scan found no publication of the finished framework. Given the benchmarking process is classified by design, absence of a public artefact is not proof the deliverable was missed — but it does mean nobody outside government can currently verify it exists.

Watch next. The first Article 91 documentation request and who receives it; whether the AI Office opens a formal proceeding against any named GPAI provider in the first weeks; whether non-signatories of the transparency Code are treated differently in practice; and whether the White House publishes any unclassified summary of the EO 14409 framework.

IMPACT 8/10#1 POLICY · COPYRIGHT

Munich rules against Suno — and rejects the text-and-data-mining defence for generative music

The Munich Regional Court ruled on 31 July that Suno infringed copyright by training its systems on works from GEMA's repertoire and by storing and reproducing them, in what is being reported as Europe's first binding decision on AI-generated music (Variety, Billboard). GEMA brought the case over Suno's use of music available on YouTube, including works associated with Alphaville. The court granted injunctive relief against reproduction of six specific works named in the case, and ordered Suno to disclose revenue connected to the unauthorised activity and pay damages (JUVE Patent). The legally consequential part is not the injunction but the reasoning: the court rejected the text-and-data-mining exception — the provision in EU Directive 2019/790 that Germany implemented as §44b of its Copyright Act — as a defence for this training (Music Ally, Music Week). GEMA's line: "AI models built on stolen intellectual property have no protection under the law." Suno says it disagrees and is evaluating its options, including appeal (TNW).

§44b is the exception most European training pipelines quietly lean on; a first-instance court has now declined to extend it to a generative model trained on a rightsholder's catalogue. This is one court, in one member state, on one modality, and it will be appealed — but if you are building on scraped media with a TDM rationale in the file, that rationale now has a published adverse decision sitting against it.

IMPACT 7/10#2 POLICY · PROVENANCE

About 190 organisations signed the EU transparency Code before the labelling rule bit

Ahead of Article 50's application, the Commission published the state of play on the Code of Practice on Transparency of AI-generated Content: roughly 190 organisations had signed by the end of July, across IT, telecoms, education and retail (European Commission). The Code was published in June 2026 as the voluntary route to demonstrating conformity with the marking and disclosure obligations (IPTC, Bird & Bird). Named signatories on the provider side include Aleph Alpha, Anthropic, Black Forest Labs, Cohere, Google, Meta, Microsoft, Mistral, OpenAI and Synthesia; on the deployer side, Bulgari, Fastweb, Getty Images, Iberdrola, Lenovo and Lufthansa. About half the signatories are small or recently founded companies — the Commission's own read on how broadly the marking obligation lands (Commission FAQ).

Signing is the cheap path to a conformity presumption, and the whole frontier field took it rather than litigate the marking rule — which tells you the provenance stack (watermarking, C2PA-style manifests, detection endpoints) is now table stakes for EU distribution, not a differentiator. If you are a deployer, note that the deployer section has its own obligations and your model vendor's signature does not cover you.

If you run a health system or a payer, assume patients are now pasting — or connecting — your records into a general-purpose assistant, and decide your position on record-sharing APIs before someone else decides it for you.

IMPACT 5/10#8 RESEARCH · BENCHMARKS

Research beat: Supabase open-sources an agent benchmark that runs against real stacks, not mocks

Announced 1 August: Supabase released Supabase Evals, an Apache-2.0 benchmark and framework measuring how well AI coding agents build against Supabase (Supabase, GitHub). It runs agents including Claude Code, Codex and OpenCode through real tasks — building a schema, debugging a failed Edge Function, fixing a broken RLS policy — and scores the result. The design choice that matters: scenarios execute against real containerised Supabase stacks rather than mocks, with scoring mixing deterministic checks and LLM-as-judge, and one retry allowed (Supabase discussion). It runs locally via pnpm, powers a public leaderboard, and doubles as an internal regression suite monitored daily (MarkTechPost).

A vendor publishing an open harness that scores agents on its own product is the emerging shape of agent evaluation — narrower than SWE-bench, executable, and maintained by whoever has to answer support tickets. The bias is obvious and the signal is still better than a leaderboard nobody can rerun.

Promoted from Tracking: AI is now the leading stated reason for US job cuts

WHAT CHANGED This arc sat as a one-liner at 322 layoff events and 205,832 workers. The attribution data is what moved: AI-linked cuts for 2026 now run at roughly 87,700 — already above the 54,836 attributed across the whole of 2025 and the 12,742 of 2024 combined — and Challenger's read is that AI has become the single most-cited reason, having led all categories for three consecutive months (Outlook Business).
The sector picture from Challenger's mid-year data: technology announced 139,156 cuts through June, up 83% from 76,214 in the same period of 2025, and accounted for close to a third of all US job-cut announcements in H1 (HR Dive, CFO Dive). Independent tracking puts 2026 to date at 322 layoff events affecting 205,832 workers, with 173 events — 54% — explicitly citing AI, automation or machine learning, touching roughly 170,945 of those workers (SkillSyncer tracker). Named actions in the window include Oracle reducing headcount by about 21,000 over the past year and Cisco cutting 4,000 roles (InformationWeek). The counter-signal worth holding: Gallup data reported this year finds non-users of AI more likely to face layoffs than users (Fox Business).

"Cited as a reason" is an employer's framing decision, not an audited causal finding — restructurings get an AI label because it reads better to markets than "we over-hired." But the direction and the size of the year-over-year step are real, and they now sit alongside Google's ATLAS finding (Tracking) that AI touches ~21% of tasks in a typical job. Both can be true: shallow task penetration, deep headcount effect at the margin.
