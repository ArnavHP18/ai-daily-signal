Amazon closes megacap week by answering the capex question the loudest way available: AWS re-accelerates to 37%

The number the whole quarter was waiting on came in well above the bar. AWS grew about 37% year over year to roughly $42.2B — its fastest expansion in eighteen quarters, against a consensus near 31% — with AWS operating income of about $16.6B at a 39.4% margin. Group revenue rose about 20% and operating income about 43%, and net income printed at about $62.6B, a figure inflated by the mark on Amazon's Anthropic stake rather than by operations. Andy Jassy said AWS is "booming" and that the company's AI and custom-chips businesses have each passed a $25B annual run rate. Then came the bill: 2026 capital expenditure was lifted to about $220B from the roughly $200B the Street had been modelling, and the AWS backlog — contracted work not yet recognised — stands at about $496B. Q3 guidance is $197–202B (CNBC, Yahoo Finance, Amazon release).

So what: the split verdict this briefing described yesterday just hardened into a rule. Microsoft held capex flat and accelerated cloud — paid. Meta raised capex and shrank operating income — charged. Amazon raised capex by $20B and accelerated cloud by nine points, and produced a $496B backlog to justify it. The test the market is applying is not "how much" but "against what contracted revenue," and Amazon is the first company this quarter to show the receipt alongside the invoice.

The line that mattered. AWS revenue of about $42.2B, up roughly 37% (36.7% on the more precise reading), is the segment's fastest growth since 2021 and its best in eighteen quarters. Consensus was near 31%; Q1 had printed 28%, which at the time was itself described as the fastest in fifteen quarters. Two consecutive quarters of acceleration is the pattern, not one surprise. AWS operating income of about $16.6B at a 39.4% margin says the acceleration is not being bought with discounting (CNBC, Yahoo Finance).

The capex number, and why it is different from Meta's. Amazon lifted its 2026 capital-expenditure plan to about $220B, roughly $20B above where the sell side had it. On its own that is the same move Meta made and was punished for. The difference is the $496B AWS backlog — contracted, not-yet-recognised work — disclosed in the same breath. A backlog of that size converts capex from a bet into a delivery obligation, which is a materially different thing to underwrite (TradingKey, Quartr transcript summary).

Silicon as a business line. Jassy's disclosure that the AI business and the custom-chips business have each passed a $25B annual run rate is the first time Amazon has sized Trainium-class silicon as a standalone revenue story rather than a cost-avoidance story. The reference point is April's ten-year, $100B arrangement to run Anthropic's models on AWS Trainium. If custom silicon is a $25B run-rate line at one hyperscaler, the second-source thesis stops being about AMD alone (Yahoo Finance).

The profit line needs an asterisk. Group net income of about $62.6B is not an operating result. As with Microsoft's $3.2B Anthropic mark last night, a large share of it is the revaluation of Amazon's Anthropic position. Advertising revenue was up 26%, and group operating income rose about 43% — those are the operating numbers to hold onto (Variety).

The guidance wrinkle. Q3 revenue guidance of $197–202B carries a self-inflicted comparison problem: Amazon moved Prime Day into June this year, pulling volume out of the July quarter. Read the guide against that, not as demand softness.

Where this leaves the arc. Four megacaps have now reported inside seven days. Microsoft: capex ~$190B, held flat, Azure +43%. Alphabet: FY26 capex $195–205B. Meta: $130–145B and free cash flow down 91%. Amazon: ~$220B with a $496B backlog. Apple, reporting the same evening, spent about $3.4B in the quarter and is on track for just over $11B for the year — an order of magnitude below the others, for a company running its AI strategy on-device. The sector's 2026 infrastructure bill now clears $700B on the aggregate estimates, and the dispersion between how those bills are being received is wider than at any point in this arc.

IMPACT 8/10#2 MODELS · PRICING

OpenAI cuts GPT-5.6 Luna by 80% and Terra by 20%, and replaces Priority Processing with a Fast mode

Effective 30 July. GPT-5.6 Luna falls to $0.20 per million input tokens and $1.20 per million output — an 80% cut. GPT-5.6 Terra falls 20% to $2 / $12 per million. OpenAI frames the move as passing through inference-efficiency gains rather than as a promotion. Alongside it, a new Fast mode in the API delivers up to 2.5× the throughput of Standard at roughly 2× the price, and supersedes the Priority Processing tier for GPT-5.6 Sol. The changelog for the same date also carries deprecations — reusable prompt objects, the Evals platform and Agent Builder — with migration timelines (OpenAI, VentureBeat, Axios).

An 80% cut at the cheap end does not change what is possible — it changes what is affordable at volume, which is where classification, extraction and routing workloads live. Reprice any per-token business case older than a quarter, and treat the Agent Builder deprecation as a build-versus-buy signal: OpenAI is retiring the visual layer and keeping the API.

IMPACT 7/10#4 POLICY · INDUSTRIAL

Europe opens bidding on up to seven AI gigafactories — €10B public, €20B private, bids close 12 November

The European Commission formally opened the AI Gigafactories call on 30 July. Up to seven facilities, backed by up to €10B of EU and national money, intended to mobilise at least €20B of private investment for a headline above €30B. Consortia or special-purpose vehicles have until 12 November to bid; awards are expected in early 2027; the Commission wants the first sites operational by mid-2028. That is the whole European bet in one week — regulate the frontier and subsidise the substrate. The bid date, 12 November, is the first real test of whether European capital shows up for the other €20B.

IMPACT 7/10#5 RESEARCH · EMBODIED AI

Google DeepMind puts a whole humanoid — legs, torso, arms, fingers — under one learned policy

Released 30 July: Gemini Robotics 2, a three-model suite. The vision-language-action model is DeepMind's first to control a full humanoid body — locomotion, balance, bending and multi-finger manipulation — under a single learned policy rather than a stack of separate controllers. Gemini Robotics ER 2 is the embodied-reasoning layer: it parses spoken instructions, decomposes them into multi-step plans, coordinates multiple robots and monitors progress to completion. Gemini Robotics On-Device 2 runs locally, removing the network round-trip, and DeepMind says it adapts to a new robot body within hours. ER 2 is in public preview through Google AI Studio and the Gemini API as gemini-robotics-er-2-preview; the VLA and on-device models remain gated to early-access partners (Google DeepMind, Google).

Whole-body control under one policy is the step that makes humanoids a software problem instead of a controls-engineering problem. Note what shipped and what did not: the reasoning layer is open to any developer today, the body-control layer is not. Google is distributing the brain and holding the spine.

IMPACT 6/10#6 BUSINESS · M&A

Nscale pays about $1.65B for Anyscale, buying the orchestration layer to sit on top of its own power and GPUs

Definitive agreement announced 30 July. Nvidia-backed Nscale — power generation, data-centre real estate and GPU clusters — is acquiring Anyscale, the commercial steward of the open-source Ray distributed-computing framework, for about $1.65B per Bloomberg's sourcing. Anyscale's roughly 200 employees across the US, Europe and India move across; the brand continues serving existing customers independently. The neoclouds have worked out that renting bare GPUs is a commodity business with a hyperscaler-sized competitor. Buying the scheduler is how you stop being one. Watch whether Ray's governance stays neutral — a lot of other people's training runs depend on it.

IMPACT 6/10#9 HARDWARE · SOVEREIGN COMPUTE

The US nuclear-weapons agency picks a developer for a 1GW AI campus with 2GW of its own generation

Dated 20 July and not previously carried here. The Department of Energy's National Nuclear Security Administration selected Amentum to enter negotiations for a phased lease to build an AI data centre plus dedicated on-site generation at the Savannah River Site in South Carolina: about 1GW of compute backed by roughly 2GW of its own power, starting on natural gas and bridging to nuclear. Federal land plus dedicated generation is a template, and the ratepayer framing is the political argument every private data centre will need by 2027.

IMPACT 4/10#11 ENTERPRISE · VERTICAL AI

Fenergo ships an agentic KYC layer built around an audit trail rather than around autonomy

Announced 29 July, the Friday vertical beat. Fen-AI is an agentic orchestration platform for client lifecycle management, KYC and AML at financial institutions. The initial release is six automation agents covering routine lifecycle work, built on Fen-X, Fenergo's legal-entity system of record, so that every agent action is written back against a governed entity record. IMPACT 9/10D2 POLICY · REGULATION

One day to the US benchmarking deadline, two to EU enforcement — and Brussels adds a €10B carrot to the stick

WHAT CHANGED The clock ticks down to T-1 and T-2: EO 14409's classified cyber-benchmarking process is due tomorrow, and on Sunday 2 August the Commission's GPAI enforcement and penalty powers switch on at the same hour California's AI Transparency Act becomes operative. New this edition: the Commission paired the enforcement date with an industrial-policy leg — the €30B AI Gigafactories call opened 30 July (New #4). Regulate the model, subsidise the substrate, in the same week.
On 1 August, the classified cyber-benchmarking process for "covered frontier models" is due from Treasury, NSA and CISA under EO 14409, alongside the reported voluntary 30-day pre-release framework with OpenAI, Google and Anthropic. On 2 August, the Commission and the AI Office gain the power to demand documentation, run technical evaluations, order risk-mitigation measures, restrict or withdraw a model from the EU market, and fine up to €15M or 3% of global turnover under Article 101 — with the Article 99 ceiling at €35M or 7% for prohibited practices. Refusing or stalling on documentation is itself finable. Models placed on the market before 2 August 2025 have until August 2027. Brussels has already named systemic cybersecurity risk as its supervisory priority (artificialintelligenceact.eu, Commission GPAI guidelines).

Three regimes with different theories of the same problem switch on inside 48 hours, one of them a US state the federal government is actively litigating to preempt. If you ship or embed a GPAI model, the artefact you need in hand on Sunday morning is the one that should already be written. Monday is when you find out who wrote it.
