The EU just regulated ChatGPT as a search engine — the first AI chatbot ever designated under the DSA, with obligations that bite by January 2027

On 1 September 2026 the European Commission designated ChatGPT a Very Large Online Search Engine (VLOSE) under the Digital Services Act, alongside Reddit and Roblox as Very Large Online Platforms. The trigger is the 45 million average monthly EU user threshold, which OpenAI's service does not merely clear but overshoots — reported at roughly 160 million. The reasoning matters more than the label: the designation turns on ChatGPT's live web-search function, treating it as a hybrid service that qualifies as an online search engine because it responds to prompts by searching the web. That is a capability test, not a product category. From designation, the company has four months — by January 2027 — to comply with the VLOP/VLOSE tier: systemic-risk assessment and mitigation covering illegal content, effects on minors, physical and mental wellbeing, fundamental rights, electoral processes and public security, plus independent audit and data access for researchers (European Commission, heise, Business Standard).

So what: for two years the live question in EU AI compliance has been how the AI Act would land. This is the answer arriving from a different statute. The DSA reached a frontier assistant first, and it did so without needing a single new AI rule — it simply found that a chatbot which searches the web is a search engine. Any assistant with a browse tool and EU scale should now assume the same test applies to it, and should be counting users against 45 million rather than waiting for an AI Act trigger.

Why this leads. Under the v2 rule the hero is the biggest genuinely new item unless a Developing thread escalated decisively, and none did. This is the item with the widest set of people who must act, and it is the only one in the edition that broke inside 24 hours. It is also the first time a generative AI assistant has been pulled inside the EU's systemic-risk regime — a regime with audits, researcher data access, and fines reaching 6% of global turnover in the DSA's enforcement tier.

The reasoning is the story, not the designation. Two readings were available to the Commission. It could have treated ChatGPT as a novel service and waited for the AI Act's own machinery. Instead it applied the DSA's existing definition and found that ChatGPT's search function makes it an online search engine — a hybrid service whose search behaviour is enough to designate the whole thing. That reasoning is portable. Gemini, Claude and Perplexity all answer prompts by searching the web; none of them is designated today, and the only thing standing between them and the same test is the user count. The threshold is 45 million EU monthly average users, and it is a disclosed number, not a discovered one — designation follows from the platform's own reporting.

What "comply by January 2027" actually contains. The VLOP/VLOSE tier is not a labelling obligation. It requires an annual systemic-risk assessment across named categories — dissemination of illegal content, negative effects on minors, on physical and mental wellbeing, on fundamental rights, on electoral processes and public security — plus proportionate mitigation measures, an independent annual audit, a repository of advertisements, data access for vetted researchers, and a crisis-response mechanism. For a chat interface, several of those have no settled implementation. What is an "advertisement repository" for an assistant that has just begun carrying ads in 31 European markets? What is an auditable recommender system when the recommender is a model? Those questions now have a deadline attached to them.

Read this against the AI Act thread this briefing has been carrying. For two editions the briefing has been unable to confirm a single reported "first EU AI Act fine" — no Commission decision, no named defendant, no wire report. Today the actual, verifiable, primary-sourced EU enforcement step arrives from the DSA instead. That is a useful correction to the shape of the risk: the near-term European regulatory exposure for a consumer AI product is more likely to arrive through platform law than through AI law, and platform law already has case history, designated regulators and a working enforcement record.

What to do this quarter, concretely. (1) If you operate a consumer-facing assistant with a web-browse capability, compute your EU monthly average users on the DSA's methodology now — the designation follows the number, and you publish the number. (2) If you are near the threshold, the four-month compliance clock starts at designation, not at your readiness; start the systemic-risk assessment before you are designated, not after. (3) If you are an enterprise buyer, add the January 2027 date to vendor reviews: a designated vendor will be making product changes in Europe, and some of them — minors handling, ad transparency, researcher data access — will surface in your integration.

The FTC and 22 states sued Amazon over $20B of undisclosed ad-auction surcharges — the first big test of algorithmic pricing liability

Filed Monday 31 August 2026 in Seattle federal court. The FTC and 22 states allege Amazon systematically overcharged advertisers by more than $20 billion since 2019 by manipulating its Sponsored Ads auctions with undisclosed "soft reserve prices" that inflated costs. The reason this lands on an AI briefing rather than a retail one: the mechanism at issue is an undisclosed parameter inside an automated auction, and the defence and the complaint together will produce the first substantial record on what a firm must disclose about a pricing algorithm it operates (CNBC, CNN, WWD/Sourcing Journal, Benzinga).

Put this beside the tracked FTC personalised-pricing item, whose comment window closes 18 September. The agency is arguing a disclosure theory in a courtroom and writing a disclosure standard in a docket at the same time. If you operate any automated pricing, bidding or ranking system with a hidden floor, reserve or multiplier, the cheap moment to document why it is disclosed the way it is, is now — and the docket is the cheaper of the two venues.

IMPACT 7/10#6 POLICY · COURTS · DEFENCE

A federal judge ordered the Pentagon's "supply chain risk" label on Anthropic removed as unlawful First Amendment retaliation

Ruled 27–28 August 2026. District Judge Rita Lin ordered the Defense Department to remove its "Supply-Chain Risk to National Security" designation of Anthropic, finding the department's actions "constituted unlawful retaliation in violation of the First Amendment" and that the company "was denied the pre-deprivation process required under the Fifth Amendment." The judge called the evidence that Anthropic posed a national security risk "slim" and wrote that "the empty invocation of national security is not a blank check to punish and retaliate against government critics." The underlying dispute: the Pentagon sought a clause permitting "any lawful use" of Claude; Anthropic refused on two stated red lines — domestic mass surveillance and lethal targeting in fully autonomous weapons without meaningful human authorisation. Context on the stakes: Claude was the first chatbot cleared for classified work, with a Claude Gov variant in intelligence-community use since 2024 (CNN, CNBC, NPR, TechCrunch).

Read this and New #2 as one fact. Winning the case did not win the shelf space: the designation is gone and Claude is still not on GenAI.mil, because a court can strike a punitive label but cannot write a contract term. The transferable lesson for any vendor with usage restrictions is that the enforceable object is the scope-of-use clause, and it is negotiated before award, not litigated after.

IMPACT 7/10#8 RESEARCH · AGENT EVALUATION

Agents' Last Exam puts frontier agents at 2.6% on its hardest tier — and every system tested scored zero there

The research beat's contribution, and the sharpest number in the edition. Agents' Last Exam (ALE), from Berkeley RDI with Snorkel, evaluates agents on long-horizon, economically valuable work rather than puzzles: more than 1,500 expert-sourced tasks spanning 55 occupations across 13 industry clusters. Reported results: frontier agent systems — Fable 5, GPT-5.5, Composer 2.5 among them — average 26% across all task tiers but only 2.6% on the hardest "last-exam" tier, where every frontier agent tested, including Fable 5, scored 0%. Performance is highly uneven by domain: computational mathematics and agriculture/environment ~55–85%, business and legal ~50–55%, education below 25% (Berkeley RDI, arXiv 2606.05405, Snorkel leaderboard).

This is the number to put in front of anyone building an automation business case on "agents can do the job." 26% overall and 2.6% at the top of the difficulty curve is a decomposition instruction, not a verdict: the tasks agents clear are the ones you should be automating this year, and the domain spread says where. Education at under 25% and mathematics at up to 85% is a bigger planning input than any aggregate score.

Industry ranking through July: technology 149,023, transportation 41,748, healthcare/products 34,426, services 23,942, government 20,752 (Challenger, Gray & Christmas, HR Dive).

If you are using this series in a board paper or a policy submission, quote the pair or quote neither. "205,000 AI-attributed cuts" and "hiring plans up 25%" come from the same firm, the same methodology and the same months; presenting one without the other is a selection choice, and it will be caught. The defensible claim is compositional — AI is reallocating headcount within and between sectors — not aggregate destruction.
