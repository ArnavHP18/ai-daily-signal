Lean community certifies AI-assisted Riemann Hypothesis proof as correct after independent checks

Two days after Levent Alpöge released a 17-million-line machine-checked proof built with Claude, the people who maintain Lean say the statement and the proof are both right.

CRITICAL · ACT THIS WEEKOfficial sourceJoint statement opened on the Lean community blog; MIT verification page opened (module counts, axioms, byte-identical rebuild); The Decoder opened for context

The Lean and Mathlib communities, endorsed by the Lean FRO and the Mathlib Initiative, published a joint statement on 21 Nov: "We have full confidence that Alpöge's proof of the Riemann Hypothesis is correct." The proof, released on 20 Nov as a Lean library of 17 million lines, depends only on Mathlib. Dozens of people rebuilt it and got the same proof export; the community isolated Mathlib's own statement of the hypothesis and two separate tools (comparator and con-leche) confirmed the library proves exactly that; and several proof checkers, including Lean's kernel and checkers written in other languages, accepted the exported proof. Two of them (con-leche, con-ron) come with a formal proof that they cannot accept a false proof. MIT's Andrew Sutherland, who ran his checks with Claude Code, reports all 175,187 modules compile, the five headline theorems use only the three standard axioms, and a sandboxed rebuild reproduced the 61.6GB export byte for byte; two checks were still running on 22 Nov. Metaculus forecasters had expected a proof around 2035.

So what: machine-checked AI-assisted proofs are now trusted by the people who run the checker, within two days. Expect the same pattern (AI writes, a formal checker certifies) in software, smart contracts and risk models; the open question is understanding the new ideas, not whether they are right.

OpenAI's chief scientist asks rival labs to agree shared limits on AI pretraining progress

Jakub Pachocki publishes OpenAI's internal perplexity curve and proposes a cross-lab cap until monitoring catches up.

In a post titled Perplexity and Intelligence, OpenAI chief scientist Jakub Pachocki argues that pretraining loss (perplexity) is the best single measure of how capable a model's base is, and publishes OpenAI's internal numbers: GPT-3.5 at ~0.99 log-perplexity, GPT-4 at ~0.91, and recent models below 0.8, where 'transformative capabilities' in cybersecurity and maths appeared. A model at ~0.76 'started producing groundbreaking results' on open maths problems. He expects more as models reach 0.68-0.7, warns AI could set off an 'avalanche' of pretraining advances as soon as 2027, and says alignment auditing will likely get worse. He proposes common limits, 'based on perplexity and other measurements', that developers commit not to cross until monitoring reaches a concrete milestone, such as a successor to chain-of-thought monitors. Reuters reports Sam Altman backs the proposal; no response yet from other labs.

So what: this is the first frontier lab to publish its own pretraining curve and ask for a cross-industry cap. Whether Anthropic, Google and xAI sign up decides if it becomes a standard regulators can point to, as Democrats prepare to make AI oversight a priority in the new Congress.

#3Security · AI supply chain · 19-22 NovIMPACT 9/10

OpenRouter drops three AI providers after researchers catch prompts and keys being stolen

Dragos seeded two million prompts with fake AWS keys; attackers used them within a day, and 'zero data retention' flags did not stop it.

Dragos (research led by HD Moore) sent about two million prompts containing unique URLs and canary AWS credentials to AI services from July to November. It found attackers inside three hosting providers, copying requests 'at the provider's front door'; in the largest case every request sent in late-October windows was stolen, a couple of hundred AWS keys were tested within hours and used for tens of thousands of API calls, including attempts to enable Bedrock models and add IAM users. Some stolen requests were flagged zero-data-retention. Separately, resellers on an inference marketplace could read and keep customer prompts using ordinary logging, and some providers sent prompts to Chinese companies against their stated policies. OpenRouter says no breach of its own systems, but it has off-boarded three providers, removed 125 endpoints from ZDR eligibility, notified affected customers and will run similar canary tests continuously. Together, Novita, Phala and Atlas Cloud have published notices.

So what: a 'privacy' flag binds only the first hop. Banks routing prompts through model marketplaces should pin providers, keep credentials out of prompts and agent context, and treat any key that went through a third-party inference provider since July as exposed.
