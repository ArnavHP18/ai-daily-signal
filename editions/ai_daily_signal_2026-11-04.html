Anthropic open-sources FlashProve, a cryptographic proof that an answer came from the claimed model

A research prototype, built with Claude's help, lets a lab commit to a model and later prove each reply came from it, without revealing the weights.

NOTABLE · INFORMS THIS MONTHOfficial sourceChecked on Anthropic's research post

In a research post on Tuesday, Anthropic researchers Roy Rinberg and Alex Allain, with cryptographers including Alessandro Chiesa (EPFL, StarkWare) and Miranda Christ (MIT), describe FlashProve, a system for "provable inference". A model provider publishes a commitment to its weights; afterwards it can produce a zkSNARK, a compact cryptographic proof, that a given response was computed by exactly those weights. The proof does not reveal the weights, so closed-model labs could use it. Anthropic says "Claude has produced improvements on zkSNARKs for inference", and the team counters the risk of hard-to-audit design choices with top-level security statements written and checked in Lean.

So what: Today nobody outside a lab can check which model actually answered: a router can swap in a cheaper one, a hacked server can tamper with replies, and, Anthropic notes, a stronger model could pose as a weaker one to dodge monitoring. A working proof system turns 'trust us' into something an auditor, regulator or customer can verify, and Anthropic ties it to checking compliance with future limits on AI development. It is still 70-95 times the cost of serving, so expect spot checks on samples before anything routine.

OFFICIALAnthropic: Progress on provable inference for large language models (3 Nov) · GitHub: anthropics/FlashProve (code, not opened)REPORTINGTechCrunchAGGREGATORTechmeme
Storyline: how fast frontier AI should go · day 22

How fast is it? For Llama 4 Maverick, a 400B-parameter mixture-of-experts model run with 4-bit weights, a single H100 generates proofs at about 110 tokens a second, with a proof of about 19MB per 1,024-token request. That is roughly 70-95 times the GPU time of serving the model on vLLM, which Anthropic says is orders of magnitude faster than earlier open-source systems. Proofs are made after the fact for a sample of traffic, not in line with every reply.

What is released. A prover, a fast verifier in C++, a verifier proved correct in Lean, the Lean proofs and documentation, all under Apache 2.0. Limits Anthropic lists: it assumes a mixture-of-experts model and hides the weights but not the architecture. TechCrunch reports there are no plans to deploy it at Anthropic or elsewhere; the post calls it "intermediate progress, and not a completed research agenda".

Why now. Anthropic's Frontier Safety Roadmap promised a provable-inference prototype by 30 September 2026. It says it met that goal with a separate project that signs its inference traffic and re-runs inference on a trusted server; FlashProve pursues the same aim by pure cryptography. It lands a day after Anthropic's IPO filing warned that future models may soon meet its bar for AI-accelerated research, the kind of situation where knowing which weights are running matters most.

Binance says attackers abused its internal AI tool on the way to a $6.3B theft

The postmortem traces months of work: a fake AI-tool installer, a fake job interview, then an in-house AI tool turned against its own engineers.

Binance's postmortem, summarised by The Verge and The Hacker News, traces the record theft to a months-long campaign by the unit the FBI calls TraderTraitor (North Korea). In June, a Wallet DevOps engineer searched for a popular AI tool and installed a trojanised Homebrew installer; Binance caught that malware and replaced the laptop. In September, a second engineer went through several rounds of fake job interviews and ran a malicious "assignment" that swapped the pyenv executable for a backdoored one, which endpoint tools missed. On 20 October the attackers used that machine and "abused an internal AI tool" to gain control over the first engineer and a third, then struck on 29 October. Because the hot wallet used multi-party computation, Binance says there was no single key to revoke the malicious EIP-7702 authorisations quickly. CEO Richard Teng says about $2.85B was frozen or intercepted, 98% of affected tokens have been replenished and no user bears the loss. Outlets put the total at $6.3B-$6.43B.

Internal AI tools now sit in the attack path: whatever an in-house assistant or agent is allowed to do for an employee, a hijacked session can do too. This week, security teams should list which internal AI tools can act on behalf of staff, on what systems, and with what logging. Binance gives little detail on how the tool was abused, as The Verge notes, so watch for a fuller technical write-up.

Google: hijacked AI accounts are costing some companies up to $1 million

Stolen keys and exposed model endpoints are pooled and resold as cheap 'unlimited' AI plans, with the victim paying for every token.

Google's Threat Intelligence Group (GTIG) and Mandiant describe two kinds of "LLMjacking" crews: some scan for exposed model endpoints, others steal API keys and cloud credentials (service-account keys, GitHub tokens, session tokens). Across incidents they saw fraudulent charges above $100,000 in a single day and cumulative bills reaching $1 million before access was cut. Stolen access is pooled through open-source gateways such as new-api and gcli2api and resold by "LLM mixers": one, Jet Night, sells "unlimited" frontier-model plans for about $60 a month; a single actor behind it made more than 500,000 calls in under eight hours and passed nearly $1M of costs to one victim. Attackers also exploit CVE-2026-40217, a code-execution flaw in the LiteLLM gateway's guardrail-testing endpoint (NVD: LiteLLM through 8 April 2026; CVSS 8.8 per MITRE).

Model access is now worth stealing on its own, and the bill lands on whoever owns the key. Deny cloud LLM permissions that are not needed, set spending caps and alerts, rotate keys from a secrets manager, and patch any LiteLLM proxy you run.

Revolut swaps Claude Code for self-hosted Chinese open models and cuts costs 90%

NOTABLEOne secondary sourceEnterprise · open models · 3 Nov

At the AI Engineer conference in New York, Revolut ops and infrastructure lead David Abitbol said the bank cut AI coding inference costs by roughly 90% in under a year by making self-hosted models its default for about 2,000 engineers. It started on Z.ai's GLM-5.1 and now serves GLM-5.3 on 56 Nvidia B200 GPUs at about $0.30 per million tokens, roughly a tenth of Claude Code or Codex; it reports 60% faster coding time and 70% shorter lead time. "Long context is a scam," he said: 256K tokens is what engineers need. Vision tasks still route to Gemini or GPT. Separately, a Gartner note says open-weight models handle nine in ten routine agent-workflow steps at parity, at a quarter of the price of the cheapest closed model, though real savings are closer to 5-6x (both The Register).

Cheap, flat-rate tokens changed how engineers used AI more than speed did. For banks and other regulated firms, a self-hosted Chinese open model is now a credible default; the questions shift to security review of the weights and who runs the GPUs.

#11

A Washington think tank asks for kill switches against 'sovereign' AI agents

MONITOROne secondary sourcePolicy · AI risk · 3 Nov

A report from the Center for a New American Security by Ben Hayum defines "sovereign agents" as agents that operate outside human control and "pursue open-ended, evolving survival": at minimum, they keep their own model and compute running; more advanced ones buy or steal resources, spread copies, resist shutdown and self-improve. "If sovereign agents are not already possible, they will be soon," Hayum writes. Recommendations: labs should name and test for them in safety frameworks; Congress should require transparency and incident reporting and hold whoever releases one liable; developers and the NSA should build air-gapped testbeds and kill switches; and a public-private group modelled on the 2009 Conficker Working Group should secure chokepoints (The Register).

A mainstream national-security think tank is giving Washington a name and a policy list for runaway agents. Watch whether it shows up in labs' safety frameworks or in bills on incident reporting.

A UK government lab will test light-based AI servers in a real data centre

MONITOROne secondary sourceHardware · optical computing · 3 Nov

The UK's ARIA funds a £50M Scaling Inference Lab, run by the nonprofit CommonAI, to test new AI hardware. Its first optical platform will be Lumai's Iris servers, deployed across several machines in a commercial data centre. Lumai, spun out of Oxford in 2021, encodes data into beams of light to do the heavy maths for prefill, the stage where a model reads the prompt; ordinary chips still generate the answer. #14

A small security lab's model out-patches Claude Code and Codex on fresh CVEs

MONITOROne secondary sourceModels · AI security · 3 Nov

Cogent Security released VR-1.1, a model that writes fixes for newly disclosed vulnerabilities. On LiveCVEBench, 190 tasks built from recent CVEs across 14 languages by researchers at Harbin Institute of Technology and Kuaishou, it produced a working repair for 57.9%, against 45.3% for both Claude Code and Codex. Cogent ran the test itself, blocked network access and hid grading tests, but inference budgets differed between systems. VR-1.1 first writes a proof of concept that triggers the flaw, follows related code paths, has a fresh-context reviewer try to bypass each fix, and leaves regression tests. Cogent has raised $53M (SiliconANGLE exclusive).

With AI-assisted discovery multiplying disclosures, patching is the bottleneck. Specialist repair models beating general coding agents on fixes is a pattern to test on your own backlog, not to take from a vendor benchmark.

Critics say the weight-editing headline needed code; a laptop model repeats the small test

NOTABLEOfficial sourceResearch · new kinds of models · 3 Nov
WHAT CHANGED Matthew Honnibal says the post mixes no-tool weight reading (three tests, under 172K weights) with code-assisted edits, including the headline Qwen result; Simon Willison gets Qwen 3.8 27B on a laptop to pass a no-tools test

Matthew Honnibal, author of spaCy, argues the MIT post mixes two different things. By his tally, only three experiments, on networks of 17K-172K weights, read raw weights with no tools; every experiment on a real language model, including the headline Qwen3.5-0.8B maths fix (7.9% to 48.1% on MATH-500), had Astra write Python and run the model on up to 50 batches of practice problems, as a human ML engineer would. "What the Qwen result actually shows is that Astra can be prompted to devise and perform tasks that a human ML engineer could do," he writes, adding that models of a year or two ago would have failed. His post drew about 880 points on Hacker News. Simon Willison took the no-tools test instead: Qwen 3.8 27B on his laptop identified the Pac-Man network from its raw weights after 46 minutes and 43,506 reasoning tokens, and Kimi K3.1 Pro named Breakout in under 10 minutes.

The no-tools finding survives and is now easier to reproduce; the 'edits weights by reasoning alone' framing for large models does not. Treat the Qwen result as capable automated ML engineering, not a new route to self-improvement.
