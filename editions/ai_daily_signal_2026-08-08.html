■ Today's Lead · New · Open weights · Day 22 of the open-frontier arc

The first open-weight model that is closed to the West — MiniMax publishes H3, then geo-fences the licence

On 3 August MiniMax pushed the weights for H3 — the omni-modal video model it had launched API-only on 31 July — to Hugging Face, with native ComfyUI support landing the same day (ComfyUI Wiki, 3 Aug, Hugging Face community write-up). The weights are real and downloadable. The rights are not universal: the MiniMax H3 Community License Agreement explicitly excludes the United States, the European Union, the United Kingdom and South Korea from local deployment (TechTimes, 4 Aug, explainx.ai). H3 is described as a 33.1B-parameter dense single-stream transformer — not a mixture-of-experts — reading text, images, video and audio in one pass on a Qwen3-VL-32B encoder backbone, generating 4–15 second clips at up to 2K/24fps with native stereo audio, and accepting up to 9 images, 3 video clips and 3 audio inputs as conditioning (RunPod). Trackers place it first in Video Editing (with audio) on Artificial Analysis and top-three in text-to-video and image-to-video.

So what: for twenty-two editions this briefing has tracked the open-weight ceiling as a capability number. Today it becomes a jurisdiction question. If you are a Western company that planned to self-host H3 — for data-residency, cost or latency reasons — the licence says you cannot, and "open weights" has stopped being a synonym for "yours to run."

What shipped, and when. Two dates matter and they are different. H3 (also carried as Hailuo 3.0) launched as a paid API product on 31 July 2026. The open weights followed on 3 August, published to Hugging Face under a bespoke community licence rather than Apache-2.0 or MIT. This is why the item is badged new and not NEW.24H: it is five days old and this briefing did not carry it before. It is new to these pages, not new to the world, and the card says so.

The architecture is the unusual part. Nearly every large open release this briefing has carded in the last month has been a mixture-of-experts — Tencent's Hy3 at 295B/21B, DeepSeek-V4-Flash at 284B/13B, Poolside's Laguna S 2.1 at 118B/8B. H3 is reported as a dense 33.1B single-stream transformer that ingests all four modalities through one pass, using Qwen3-VL-32B as the encoder backbone. Dense at 33B is a size a serious workstation can hold; that is precisely what makes the licence restriction bite, because self-hosting was genuinely on the table.

The licence is the story. Every export-control thread this briefing has carried for the last month ran one direction: Washington and Brussels restricting what Chinese labs can buy, and MOFCOM consulting Alibaba, ByteDance and Z.ai about restricting overseas access to Chinese models. H3's Community License is the first instance carded here of a Chinese lab writing that restriction into the artefact itself, at release, by naming four Western jurisdictions and excluding them from local deployment rights. It arrives in the same week that roughly $35B is being raised across 60+ new China-focused dollar funds — the item this briefing carded on 7 August. The capital and the licensing are pointing the same way.

What is not established here. This scan did not read the licence text itself. The exclusion is reported consistently by two outlets and a community explainer; the operative clause, its definition of "local deployment," and whether API access from excluded jurisdictions is also barred were not verified against the file on Hugging Face. The 33.1B parameter count, the $0.13-per-second API price and the Artificial Analysis placements are all secondary reporting, not first-party. Treat the exclusion as well-sourced and the mechanics as unread.

Watch next. Whether Hugging Face gates the repository by region or leaves enforcement to the licence; whether any Western enterprise publicly states it is deploying H3 anyway; and whether the next major Chinese open release — Alibaba's Qwen3.8-Max weights, due in the window that opens on 10 August and carded in Developing below — carries a comparable clause. That is the single highest-value test of whether this is MiniMax's idiosyncrasy or a new default.

OpenAI moves to dismiss Apple's trade-secrets suit: "no use, need or desire for Apple's trade secrets"

Filed Wednesday 5 August, reported 6 August. In a 31-page motion, OpenAI asks the court to throw out Apple's suit — which accuses OpenAI and two ex-Apple hires of trade-secret theft and of using job interviews to extract confidential information — arguing Apple failed to describe the information it claims as trade secrets, did not show it owned a protectable secret, and did not plausibly allege misappropriation by any defendant (Bloomberg, Axios). The 17 August date is the one to diary, not the October hearing — a preliminary injunction is the only mechanism here that could constrain OpenAI's hardware work before trial. Everything else is pleading.

IMPACT 5/10#10 RESEARCH · EVALUATION

Benchmarks last because experts curated them, not because the test set stayed secret

"When AI Benchmarks Plateau: A Systematic Study of Benchmark Saturation" (arXiv 2602.16763), updated 6 August 2026, studies how benchmarks lose discriminating power over time. Its central finding cuts against the field's working assumption: resilience to saturation is driven by expert curation, not by keeping test data private. Design choices, the authors argue, can measurably extend a benchmark's useful life — which reframes benchmark construction as an editorial problem rather than a leakage-control problem.

The research leg of this edition, and it lands on the same nerve as WorldExam and the reproduction-benchmark cluster this briefing tracks: every "state of the art" claim you read is only as durable as the curation behind the ruler. Held-out data is not the moat.
