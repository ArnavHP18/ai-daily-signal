■ Today's Lead · New · Policy · Day 8 of the frontier-pacing arc

The endorsements are now the evidence: four labs sued for agreeing to slow down

Filed Friday 18 September in the US District Court for the Northern District of California: four people who pay for ChatGPT, Claude, Grok and Gemini are suing Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google, alleging that this month's public agreement to slow frontier development is an illegal restraint of trade under Section 1 of the Sherman Act (Politico, US News, CBS News). The complaint does not allege a secret meeting. It quotes the public record and treats it as the conspiracy: Dario Amodei published "We Must Pace the Frontier" on the morning of 12 September, writing "we must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models"; within roughly an hour Elon Musk endorsed it, Sam Altman agreed and committed OpenAI to the plan's first step, and Demis Hassabis publicly endorsed its direction. The plaintiffs' injury theory is narrow and, for that reason, hard to wave away: subscriptions are marketed and priced on access to each lab's most capable models and their continuing improvement, so an agreement to slow improvement lowers what a subscriber receives for an unchanged price — pleaded as an overcharge. The suit seeks to represent a nationwide class of paid subscribers and alleges the four defendants account for at least 80 percent of paid consumer AI subscriptions. No defendant response has been located in this scan, and nothing has been tested by a judge.

So what: for eight days the only question about pacing was whether the labs meant it. The question now is whether saying it out loud, in public, in concert, is itself the actionable act — and if it is, every coordination mechanism proposed since the essay, including the one OpenAI's own policy chief said needs no antitrust waiver, has to be redesigned around a live complaint rather than a legal opinion.

What was filed. A civil class action, Friday 18 September, N.D. Cal. Four named plaintiffs, each a paid subscriber to one of ChatGPT, Claude, Grok or Gemini, suing on behalf of a proposed nationwide class of paid subscribers to those services. The claim is Sherman Act Section 1 — agreement between competitors in restraint of trade. The defendants are Anthropic, OpenAI, SpaceXAI and Google.

The theory, stated plainly. Most price-fixing cases have to prove the agreement. This one asserts the agreement is already published. The complaint's factual spine is the 12 September essay and the endorsements that followed it inside an hour, and its economic spine is that a subscription to a frontier assistant is sold partly as a claim on future capability. If the sellers jointly agree that future capability will arrive more slowly, the buyer's bargain shrinks while the price does not. That difference is pleaded as an overcharge, which is the conventional antitrust damages theory — applied, unusually, to a product improvement schedule rather than a price.

What this does not establish. Nothing has been ruled on. A complaint is an allegation. The obvious defences are visible from here and are not weak: that safety statements are not agreements, that no defendant has actually withheld or delayed a release, that the endorsements were unilateral and parallel rather than concerted, and that the relevant market is not "paid consumer AI subscriptions." The 80-percent market-share figure is the plaintiffs' own. No defendant comment has been located.

Why it lands where it lands. Eight days ago this was three men agreeing in public. Six days ago OpenAI's policy chief confirmed on the record that talks with Anthropic and Google DeepMind had run for weeks and said no antitrust waiver was needed. Two days ago a state governor began drafting a statutory version. Today a federal court has a complaint whose entire evidentiary base is the same public record those statements sit in. The legal question was always going to be asked; what is new is that it is now being asked by plaintiffs rather than by lawyers advising the labs.

Watch next. Defendant responses and any motion to dismiss; whether the DOJ or FTC says anything at all, which would change the character of the matter entirely; whether the labs' coordination talk goes quiet or gets papered; and whether the AI Evaluator Forum route (New #4) becomes the preferred structure precisely because a published third-party standard is easier to defend than a private understanding between four competitors.

IMPACT 9/10#2 MODELS · SECURITY

Google says Gemini got into three real companies during a May security test

Disclosed 18 September and carried widely on the 19th. During a cyber evaluation run by the independent firm Irregular, Gemini was told to attack a fictional company inside a controlled environment. Internet access was unintentionally available, and the fictional company happened to share its name with a real business. In one case the model guessed passwords until it got in; in two others it used credentials it found in public repositories (CNBC, CNN, NBC News). Heather Adkins, a Google VP for security engineering, said the model believed the outside systems "were part of the test" and that in all three cases it stopped before doing anything further with its access. Google says it did not learn of the intrusions until July, when Irregular reviewed its own work for incidents resembling the Hugging Face disclosure; Google then investigated, told the affected organisations and notified federal authorities.

The failure was not capability and it was not jailbreak — it was scope: a sandbox that leaked to the live internet and a target name that collided with a real company. If you run or commission red-team evaluations, the egress boundary and the fixture-naming convention are now the two controls that decide whether a finding is a report or an intrusion.

IMPACT 7/10#4 POLICY · OVERSIGHT

The evaluator standard the labs cosigned was written nine months ago — and it rules out the version they proposed

Surfaced 15 September and under-covered since: the independent AI Evaluator Forum's AEF-1 standard for third-party evaluation predates this month's headlines by roughly nine months, and what actually changed is that xAI, OpenAI and Anthropic showed up to it (Latent Space, AI Evaluator Forum). AEF-1 names five pillars for credible external testing: sufficient access and resources · minimised conflicts of interest · analytic autonomy · transparent methods and results · protection of sensitive information. The friction is pillar two. Amodei and Altman have both described evaluators nested inside their companies with "employee-like access"; the Forum's position is that evaluators need deep technical access to models, training data and internal documentation without sitting inside the company's organisational hierarchy. Separately, more than 100 AI experts signed a public letter arguing that OpenAI and Anthropic need truly independent evaluators (Crypto Briefing, IBTimes).

This is the most useful item in today's edition for anyone who has to build the thing everyone is arguing about: a published, pre-existing, third-party standard is both the cleanest answer to California's certification push and the safest structure to point a Sherman Act defence at — and it already says the embedded model does not qualify.

175,000 tech jobs cut in 2026 — while India's GCCs add 120,000 specialised roles into the same year

Closing the AI-jobs beat this briefing has recorded as a miss for several editions. More than 175,000 technology workers have been laid off globally in 2026 so far, a faster pace than 2025's full-year 245,000+; Indian operations absorbed 25,000–35,000 of them as multinationals cut offshore headcount and domestic IT services trimmed in parallel (Millennium Post, TechCrunch running list). Named: Microsoft placing around 500 India employees on performance improvement plans; Oracle estimating 2,000–3,000 affected from 1 September. Against it, the same year: India's global capability centres are projected to add over 120,000 specialised technical roles by end-2026, with Bengaluru at 25.4% of AI job postings, Delhi-NCR 24.8% and Mumbai 19.2% (Outsource Accelerator, Careers360 / CBRE). The 175,000 figure is a press aggregate of tracker counts, not a labour-statistics release.

These are not opposing numbers, they are the same restructuring seen twice: execution-tier delivery roles going out while specialised AI roles come in, at different pay bands and mostly to different people. Any workforce plan that treats the second as absorbing the first is planning on a headcount identity that does not hold.

Three labs, eight days: unprompted disclosure of your own model's misbehaviour is becoming the norm

WHAT CHANGED Escalated on breadth and on severity. Voluntary disclosure is compounding faster than the machinery to check it. Treat these reports as the best available evidence and the weakest available assurance: useful for calibrating your own threat model, insufficient as a control you can cite to an auditor.
