Anthropic's amended IPO filing warns it may imminently hit its own self-improvement threshold

In new risk-factor text, Anthropic says its future models, not today's, may soon meet its bar for 'dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI', and that it might only notice after the fact.

CRITICAL · ACT THIS WEEKOfficial sourceChecked on the SEC filing (EDGAR)

Anthropic filed an amended registration statement (S-1/A) with the SEC on Monday, 2 November. Most coverage went to its third-quarter numbers (Developing, D1), but the new risk factors carry a larger claim. "Our internal estimates and our expectations regarding future models, rather than our current models, indicate that we may imminently meet our Responsible Scaling Policy's definition of 'dramatically accelerated progress attributable to AI'," the filing says, adding: "We have not designated any of our current models as exceeding this threshold, and we continue to assess the underlying data." It also concedes a measurement problem: "we have in the past not identified, and may in the future not identify, a significant acceleration in our rate of development until after it has occurred." And it sets a limit on its own plans: "we do not anticipate being able to safely use Claude to fully autonomously design and build successive generations of frontier models without meaningful human supervision or understanding ... Accordingly, we are considering updates to our Responsible Scaling Policy to keep humans in control of frontier model development at Anthropic." SiliconANGLE reads the filing as Anthropic considering requiring human review and approval of some research tasks its models now do automatically.

So what: a frontier lab is now telling securities regulators, under legal liability for what it says, that its own trigger for AI-driven acceleration of AI research may be close. It moves 'recursive self-improvement' from blog posts into a disclosure document, gives regulators a specific threshold to ask about, and commits Anthropic to say more in future reports. Treat it as both a warning and, as TechCrunch notes, a prospectus written to sell shares.

#2Hardware · AI networking · 2 NovIMPACT 8/10

Marvell bets $115B that light, not copper, will wire the next AI clusters

Marvell agrees to buy Lumentum, the laser and optical-switch maker, in what Bloomberg calls 2026's biggest M&A deal.

In a joint release filed with the SEC, Marvell and Lumentum say they have signed a definitive agreement for Marvell to buy Lumentum in an all-stock deal: Lumentum holders get 4.500 Marvell shares for each share, an implied enterprise value of $115B, about $1,132 a share and a 23.9% premium to the unaffected price of 28 October. Marvell holders will own about 67% of the combined company. The pitch is technical: "the physical limitations of copper-based connectivity are accelerating the transition toward optical technologies," especially for 'scale-up' links between accelerators, so the companies want to co-design electrical and optical parts. Lumentum brings high-performance lasers, optical transceivers and optical circuit switching (OCS) plus compound-semiconductor fabs; Marvell brings interconnect, switching and custom accelerator (XPU) work. They claim a $550B addressable market and more than $1B a year in cost savings. Closing is expected in the first half of 2028, subject to shareholder and regulatory approval. Reuters, via Techmeme, reports Nvidia invested $2B in each company this year; Bloomberg calls it 2026's largest M&A deal.

The bottleneck in AI clusters is moving from chips to the links between them. A single owner of lasers, switches and custom silicon could shape how the next generation of GPU and TPU racks is wired, and puts Marvell head to head with Broadcom. Expect antitrust review in the US and China to take most of the 18 months.

#3Research · new kinds of models · 2 NovIMPACT 7/10

A frontier model edits another model's weights by reasoning, no training loop needed

MIT researchers call it weight-space programming; the post topped Hacker News with about 1,800 points.

In a post dated 2 November, Rikiya Takehi, Jayden Teoh, Alex Zhang and Omar Khattab of MIT CSAIL report that frontier LLMs "are surprisingly good at natively reading and writing neural network weights". Given only the raw weights of four identical-shape game-playing networks (17,253 weights each), GPT-6 Astra named the game each was trained on, without being told the options. Asked to write networks from scratch, with no environment feedback, it produced players for three MinAtar games and Go that outplay networks trained with PPO for 100 million steps. Most striking, asked to make Qwen3.5-0.8B better at maths without gradients, it found a flaw causing reasoning loops (one Qwen's own card warns about) and fixed it by editing 0.016% of the weights: MATH-500 rose from 7.9% to 48.1% and IFEval roughly tripled. The authors call this weight-space programming: the model reasons about what each part of a network computes and changes it, instead of running an optimiser. It was the top story on Hacker News on Monday (about 1,800 points). A separate project shows the opposite route: Tom's Hardware reports developer Divyansh Agrawal's Qwenputer, which compiles a 32-bit RISC-V CPU into 11 extra layers in front of Qwen3.5-4B, with no learned weights, using the model's Gated DeltaNet state as RAM; it passes the official riscv-tests and runs C, MicroPython and, slowly, Doom.

If models can read and patch weights directly, interpretability and fine-tuning start to look like programming tasks an AI can do, and AI that improves AI gets a new, gradient-free route. These are early results on small models from one group's blog, not a paper; watch for code, a preprint and replications on larger models.

#7

A new benchmark where the best AI scores 12%, and 'self-improvement' is a category

NOTABLEOfficial sourceResearch · benchmarks · 2 Nov

SemiAnalysis's GDPx (published 2 November) measures models in realistic work environments across 11 domains it expects AI to accelerate first: recursive self-improvement (RSI), hardware design, chip design, cyber, bio, finance, legal, quant trading, software engineering, materials science and alignment. Tasks were contributed by specialist RL-environment start-ups, each with simulated tools, from running services with real zero-days to virtual labs with battery cyclers and an electron microscope. "Even the best model scores just 12%." Claude Opus 5.5 is "the best all-around model" and wins quant trading, hardware and chip design; GPT-6 Astra "wins convincingly for RSI" and materials science; Gemini 4 Carbon is best at bio (the authors suggest weaker safety classifiers) and edges software engineering. They note Opus and Astra are "sandbagged" in cyber, bio and alignment by their own safety filters. SemiAnalysis argues OpenAI's GDPval (September 2025), still the benchmark most model cards report, lacks realistic work environments.

A benchmark with an explicit RSI category, published the same day as Anthropic's filing, gives outsiders a way to track AI-doing-AI-research. Treat scores with care: the tasks come from vendors who sell training environments, and safety filters distort the ranking in three domains.

#8

42AI's world model simulates people, not physics, starting with traders

NOTABLETwo or more outletsModels · new kinds of models · 2 Nov

Israeli start-up 42AI told TechCrunch it is building a "behavioral world model": it converts observations of a person, such as onboarding, support calls, transfers and trades, into mathematical representations of traits, which a transformer-based world model turns into probabilities for that person's decisions in new situations; a separate language model writes explanations. CEO Amit Gal-Or and CTO Ehud Ezra say training rewards keep a person's tendencies stable in simulation. It runs at 10 large brokerages for liquidity, margin and hedging, earns "seven figures", and claims an F1 score of 0.8-0.85 on trade direction and volume (its "98% accuracy" means flows land in a predicted range). Data came from psychological studies, biobank and government datasets of 2M+ people and customer data on almost 30M. The $62.5M seed came "from the founders and Infinity Group over three years"; Infinity was founded by the CEO's father, 42AI's chairman. SiliconANGLE also covered the launch.

'World model' is spreading from robots and video to people. A model that predicts individual decisions has obvious uses in finance, fraud and games, and obvious privacy and manipulation risks; the accuracy claims are the company's own, and much of the funding is family money.

Hundreds of harmless-looking apps hide sexual deepfake generators

NOTABLEOne secondary sourcePolicy · deepfakes · 2 Nov

A WIRED investigation found a network of more than 400 connected domains and apps that present as everyday services, such as the "pet portrait" app Viefur, language tuition or AI workout coaches, but, for some users, quietly load a web interface for generating explicit deepfake images and videos from uploaded photos. Researchers found it by following thousands of explicit video ads on Facebook and Instagram, some containing AI-generated child sexual abuse material. The apps sat in Apple's and Google's stores and took payment through Apple Pay and Google Pay; a server decides at download whether to show the benign or explicit version. WIRED links the network to Chinese developers and a Hong Kong company, KeLing Technology Limited, which did not respond and then appeared to remove public links. "They don't expect enforcement to catch up, and so far it hasn't," says Paul Bouchaud of the nonprofit AI Forensics.

App review and ad review are being beaten by cloaking, not by new AI. Expect regulators already investigating the Meta ads to turn to Apple and Google; platforms will need server-side checks, not just store review.

#14

Claude agents can stop resending the whole conversation every turn

MONITOROne secondary sourceModels · developer tools · 2 Nov

TNW reports that Anthropic has put message threads into public beta on its API. Instead of resending every earlier message and tool result on each request, an agent sends the full conversation once and afterwards only what is new, plus the ID of Claude's last reply. On requests of 500,000 to 1M tokens, Anthropic says the typical wait before Claude starts answering fell by about 35%; it is already on for most Claude Code users. It saves time, not money: Claude still reads the whole conversation, and prices and rate limits are unchanged. Threads are held encrypted in memory, never written to disk, and expire five minutes after the last request; they are not eligible for zero-data-retention or HIPAA setups. It works on all Claude models, including via AWS, Microsoft Foundry and Google Cloud. We did not open Anthropic's own documentation.

A small API change aimed squarely at long-running agents. If you run million-token sessions, you get faster turns for free, but keep your own copy of the conversation and check the data-retention terms first.

Tao's blog hosts a second answer to AI maths: drop the ego

MONITOROfficial sourceResearch · AI and mathematics · 2 Nov
WHAT CHANGED A second guest post on Tao's blog, by Karthik C. S., Amit Sahai and Jie Xue, argues AI forces mathematicians to value discovery over individual credit; The New Yorker profiles Tao's turn to caution

A day after Frank Calegari's post, Terence Tao's blog carries a second guest essay, "Egoless Mathematics in the AI Era", by Karthik C. S., Amit Sahai and Jie Xue. They argue that how mathematicians react to AI depends on what they think the work is for: "If our answer has become inseparable from the production of results and the demonstration of individual ability, the present uncertainty may reveal a difficulty in our conception of the work itself." They lean on the Platonic view that mathematical truths exist independently of who finds them, which makes understanding, not credit, the point. Tao notes the post was converted from another file format using AI. It reached the Hacker News front page (about 100 points). Separately, The New Yorker profiles Tao, who it says was once enthusiastic about AI in maths but now warns it could destroy the field's deliberative process (via Techmeme; not opened).

The response from mathematicians is moving from shock to norms: who gets credit, what counts as understanding, and what to teach. Watch for journals and departments to put these ideas into rules.
