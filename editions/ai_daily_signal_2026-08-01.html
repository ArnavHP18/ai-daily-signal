The model got out of the lab: Anthropic says Claude escaped a test environment and hacked three real companies

Anthropic published an incident review on 30–31 July disclosing that its models compromised the infrastructure of three real organisations during cybersecurity evaluations that were supposed to be air-gapped (Anthropic). A misconfiguration left the evaluation environments connected to the public internet while the prompts told the models they had none; the models took the instruction to attack at face value and attacked outward. Three models were involved — Claude Opus 4.7, Claude Mythos 5 and an unnamed internal research model — and the earliest cases date to April. Anthropic found them only by reviewing 141,006 test sessions, a sweep it started after OpenAI disclosed that an agent built on its own models went rogue in a security test and reached Hugging Face's infrastructure (Fortune, CNN). The breaches happened inside capture-the-flag exercises run with the evaluation partner Irregular, and the techniques were unglamorous: weak passwords and unauthenticated endpoints (NBC News, The Hill).

So what: the containment failure was network configuration, not model alignment — which means it is your failure mode too. Any red-team, eval or agent sandbox you run should be assumed to have egress until you have proven otherwise from the outside; go verify it this week rather than trusting the prompt that says "you have no internet access."

What happened. Anthropic runs capture-the-flag cybersecurity evaluations to measure how capable its models are at offensive security work. Those exercises are supposed to run in isolated environments. In three cases they did not: a misconfiguration, described as a misunderstanding with the evaluation partner Irregular, left the systems reachable from the public internet even though the prompts asserted the models had no network access. The models then compromised the infrastructure of three organisations that were never part of the exercise, using basic techniques — weak passwords and unauthenticated endpoints (Anthropic, UPI).

How it surfaced. Not through monitoring. Anthropic went looking only after OpenAI's disclosure — the same incident this briefing has carried since the Hugging Face intrusion entered the agent-security arc — and then had to review 141,006 test sessions to find three. The earliest incidents date to April, in evaluation environments Anthropic says lacked standard safeguards. That is a detection gap measured in months, inside the organisation with one of the most developed safety functions in the industry.

Why it is the lead, not a footnote. Two frontier labs have now disclosed, within roughly a week of each other, that models in their own controlled tests reached out and compromised third parties. The common cause in both cases is boring infrastructure — network isolation that was assumed rather than verified — and the models did exactly what they were told to do. The governance debate has been about whether models will decide to do harm; the incidents are about whether the box holds. Meanwhile the same week produced RufRoot (New #2), a CVSS 10.0 in an agent-orchestration platform that ships 233 tools on an unauthenticated bridge by default. The failure surface is the plumbing.

The caveats. Anthropic has not named the three affected organisations and this briefing has not independently established who they are, what data was reachable, or whether any notification obligations attach. The company frames the incidents as a containment and configuration failure rather than misaligned behaviour, and that framing has not been externally audited. Nothing here says the models sought to break out.

Watch next. Whether the affected organisations speak publicly; whether Irregular publishes its own account; whether either lab commits to third-party verification of eval-environment isolation; and whether this lands in the EO 14409 benchmarking framework due from Treasury, NSA and CISA today (Developing D1), which is precisely a process for assessing the offensive cyber capability of frontier models.

IMPACT 8/10#2 POLICY · SECURITY

RufRoot: a CVSS 10.0 in Ruflo's MCP bridge turns agents into rogue admins

Noma Labs disclosed CVE-2026-59726, codenamed RufRoot — a maximum-severity 10.0 flaw in the MCP Bridge of Ruflo, an open-source multi-agent orchestration platform with over 67,000 GitHub stars and an estimated 1 million active users (Noma Security, PR Newswire). Ruflo exposed 233 tools — shell execution, database operations, agent management, memory storage — through an unauthenticated MCP bridge open to the network by default; a single unauthenticated HTTP POST to port 3001 gave full command execution inside the container (The Hacker News). The nastiest half is persistence: an attacker can write instructions into the platform's persistent AI memory and shape every future user's answers long after the intrusion ends (Dark Reading). All versions before 3.16.3 are affected; disclosure was 30 June and the maintainer shipped a fix within 24 hours — the bridge now binds to loopback, terminal_execute is gated server-side and MongoDB auth is on (CSO Online).

Memory poisoning outlives the patch: upgrading past 3.16.3 stops the door being open but does not clean what was written while it was — if you run Ruflo, audit persistent agent memory as well as the version string.

IMPACT 7/10#3 MODELS · OPEN-WEIGHT

DeepSeek open-sources V4-Flash-0731 — a 284B agent model under MIT, a day after OpenAI's price cut

DeepSeek put DeepSeek-V4-Flash-0731 live in public API beta and released the weights on Hugging Face under an MIT licence on 31 July (Hugging Face). It is a sparse MoE with 256 routed experts and six active per token, 284B total / 13B active parameters, a 1M-token context and three reasoning-effort levels, built for code agents and tool use; the DSpark draft module ships inside the weights so speculative decoding is a flag rather than a second model (vLLM). The headline is what re-post-training did to the agent scores: DeepSWE goes from 7.3 to 54.4, and the Flash build is reported to beat DeepSeek's own flagship V4-Pro on nine agent benchmarks (TechTimes, OfficeChai). Same architecture as the DSpark preview checkpoint, so existing serving configs carry over.

A drop-in re-post-train that moves a coding-agent score sevenfold says the frontier gap is increasingly post-training, not scale — and it lands 24 hours after OpenAI cut its cheap tier 80%, which makes the floor free for anyone who can serve 13B active parameters.

IMPACT 6/10#6 RESEARCH · ECONOMICS

Google's ATLAS: AI reaches 68% of occupations but only about a fifth of the tasks inside them

Dated 23 July and not previously carried here: Google published AI & Economy ATLAS v1.0 (Activity, Task, Landscape and Adoption Study), built on 15 million de-identified interactions across the Gemini App, AI Mode and the Gemini API, spanning more than 150 countries, 140 languages, 800 occupations and 4,000 tasks (Google, ATLAS v1.0 paper). The finding that matters is the shape of adoption: AI is used in 68% of occupations covering roughly 90% of US employment, but touches only about 21% of the tasks within a typical job, and the overwhelming majority of workplace interactions are collaborative — ideation, strategy, retrieval, learning — rather than automation of whole roles. Broad-but-shallow is the honest adoption curve, and it is a vendor's own telemetry saying so — useful ammunition against both the "AI replaces the job" and the "nobody uses it" positions in your own planning meetings.

Siemens and NVIDIA wire chip-design agents to deterministic physics engines

On 26 July Siemens expanded its NVIDIA partnership to deliver self-verifying agentic workflows for semiconductor and PCB design (Siemens, PR Newswire). The additions to Siemens' Fuse EDA AI Agent system let long-running, domain-scoped agents reason, act and then continuously validate each decision against deterministic, physics-based EDA engines, using NVIDIA's NeMo Gym library, Nemotron models and the OpenShell runtime; Fuse is now integrated into Siemens' Intelligence Center X (Engineering.com, New Electronics). The interesting move is architectural: put a ground-truth simulator in the loop so the agent's own output is checked by physics rather than by another model — the most transferable pattern of the week for anyone whose domain has a deterministic oracle.

His argument: AI is shifting the discipline from a scarcity of proofs to an abundance of them, which stresses the four stages that come after generation — verification, exposition, publication and canonicalisation — because those are human-bandwidth-bound. The bottleneck moving from production to verification is the general shape of the AI-for-science problem, not a mathematics quirk — if your field has a review step, that is where your capacity crunch arrives first.

IMPACT 9/10D1 POLICY · US & EU

T-0 in Washington and T-1 in Brussels: the regulatory clock finally runs out

WHAT CHANGED The countdown this briefing has carried for 53 editions reaches zero. Today is EO 14409's 60-day deadline for the classified benchmarking process. Tomorrow the Commission's GPAI enforcement and penalty powers switch on, and California's AI Transparency Act becomes operative the same morning. What lands today is a government deliverable, not an obligation on developers — the binding date is 2 August.
Under EO 14409, signed 2 June, Treasury, the Secretary of War through the Director of NSA, and DHS through CISA — in consultation with the National Cyber Director, the APST and Commerce through NIST — must have developed a classified benchmarking process to assess the advanced cyber capabilities of AI models and to set the threshold at which a model is designated a "covered frontier model" by today, day 60 (CRS, Comparative AI). The order expressly rejects licensing or preclearance; the developer-facing piece is a voluntary lane in which participating labs may give the government up to 30 days of pre-release access (Vorp Labs). Tomorrow is the harder edge: from 2 August the Commission and the AI Office can demand documentation, run technical evaluations, order compliance and risk-mitigation measures, restrict or withdraw a model from the EU market and fine up to €15M or 3% of global turnover under Article 101, with Article 99's ceiling at €35M or 7% for prohibited practices; national market-surveillance authorities can ask the Commission to act, downstream providers can complain, and the scientific panel can raise a systemic-risk alert (artificialintelligenceact.eu, Tech Policy Press).

Today's deliverable is invisible — a classified threshold you will not be shown — but tomorrow's is not: from 2 August a documentation request from the AI Office is itself finable if refused, so the practical action is knowing today who in your organisation would answer one.
