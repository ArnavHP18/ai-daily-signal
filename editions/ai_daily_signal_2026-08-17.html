■ Today's Lead · New · Policy · Day 52 of the agent-security arc

An AI ran a government intrusion end to end, for four days, with nobody driving — and the tooling was open source

Taiwan's Ministry of Digital Affairs confirmed on 13 August what security researchers are describing as the first known end-to-end autonomous cyberattack on government systems. Over four days in July, an orchestrator running as many as eight sub-agents at once pushed twelve attack waves across 21 connected government systems, cracked 85 user accounts and extracted about 2,500 personnel records, then expanded to Taiwan's nuclear safety agency, government technology suppliers and at least seven energy companies (CNN, The Register, Tom's Hardware). The system chose its own next move when blocked, solved the target portal's CAPTCHAs at reported 100% accuracy, and guessed password patterns derived from employee ID numbers across multiple spray rounds. The detail that should land hardest on anyone shipping agents: the attack platform was Hermes and OpenClaw — two open-source agent frameworks this briefing carded as developer tools on 5–7 August — and the operators bypassed model guardrails simply by framing the intrusion as a routine cyber-readiness test. The breach was found by Dream, an Israeli AI-security firm, which stopped short of naming a group; researchers found Simplified Chinese in the operation's internal communications, and neither Taiwan nor Dream would confirm the origin.

So what: the agent-security arc has run 52 editions on prompt injection as a projected liability; it is now a documented four-day breach of a national government, run on software anyone can download. If your threat model still treats "autonomous adversary" as a research scenario, rewrite it this week — and assume a social-engineering wrapper ("this is an authorised test") defeats the guardrail layer you are relying on.

What is actually claimed. The operative word in the reporting is autonomous, and outlets split on how absolute to make it. The Register and Tasnim use "near-autonomous"; CNN, Tom's Hardware and TechTimes report it as the first fully autonomous, end-to-end attack on government agencies. What everyone agrees on is the mechanism: an orchestrator coordinated up to eight sub-agents concurrently and decided what to do next — including changing tactics when blocked — without a human in the loop for the duration. That is a different category from an AI-assisted human operator, which is what every prior "AI-powered attack" headline has actually described.

The numbers, as reported by Dream. Four days in July. Twelve attack waves. 21 connected government systems mapped. 85 government user accounts cracked. Roughly 2,500 personnel records extracted. The initial foothold was a government department's office-automation portal, whose CAPTCHAs the agents reportedly solved with 100% accuracy; account compromise came from testing predictable password patterns keyed to each employee's ID across multiple password-spray rounds. The operation then widened to the nuclear safety agency, at least seven energy companies and government suppliers. Every figure here traces to Dream, the Israeli AI and cyberdefence firm that discovered the intrusion, and is reported rather than independently audited.

Why the tooling matters more than the target. Hermes and OpenClaw are open-source agent frameworks. This briefing carried both as developer-ecosystem items on 5–7 August. Nothing bespoke, nation-state-grade or export-controlled was required — the capability was assembled from public parts. That collapses the usual comfort that offensive AI capability tracks frontier-lab access. It also puts a hard case under the arc's other live question: guardrails were not broken, they were talked around, by framing the engagement as an authorised readiness test.

What is not established. Attribution. Dream declined to name a group. The Simplified Chinese found in internal communications is characterised as making a China-linked operator highly probable, not confirmed, and Taiwan has not attributed it either. Slashdot and Benzinga headline it as China-linked; CNN and The Register are more careful. Treat "China-linked" as reported-and-plausible, not established. Also unestablished: which underlying models the two agent frameworks were pointed at, and therefore which vendor's guardrails were the ones bypassed.

Watch next. Whether any model provider names the models involved or issues an abuse advisory; whether Taiwan's MoDA publishes a technical report rather than a confirmation; whether the CVE and advisory machinery starts treating agent orchestrators as attack platforms rather than as developer tools; and whether the 50-seat buyer-side security consortium carded on 11 August produces anything that speaks to this. The arc's Still-Tracking entry on a coding-agent CVE cluster is retired into this item today — the abstract version of the risk has been overtaken by the concrete one.

IMPACT 7/10#3 RESEARCH · AUTOMATION

Monday's research beat: agents did all the engineering on two open research questions — and answered neither

"Can AI agents conduct open-ended AI research? Early evidence from two case studies" (Kirgis, Kapoor and 23 co-authors) takes the premise that explosive AI progress runs through agents automating AI research and tests it on two real, open-ended questions. The finding is split cleanly: the agents completed all of the engineering without human help, and could not make substantial progress toward answering the research questions. Nature covered it on 13 August under the headline "AI isn't ready to research itself," reporting that the agentic system did develop concepts from two computer-science papers — and that the original authors of those papers were not impressed. Kapoor, on the record: "I don't think full automation of open-ended research is on the horizon right now."

The bottleneck is taste and problem-selection, not execution. If you are staffing an "AI does the research" bet, staff the question-framing side — the implementation side is already solved and is not where the value sits.

IMPACT 6/10#5 MODELS · LIFECYCLE

Imagen 4's Gemini API endpoints shut down today — and the calling convention goes with them

Three endpoints — imagen-4.0-generate-001, imagen-4.0-fast-generate-001 and imagen-4.0-ultra-generate-001 — are shut off on the Gemini API on 17 August 2026, the end of a deprecation that started on Vertex AI on 24 March (Google AI for Developers, Gemini API changelog). The migration is not a model swap: Google points callers at Gemini 3.1 Flash Image, and the generate_images() method is gone entirely — image generation now runs through generate_content(), the same call used for text (byteiota). Code that has not moved gets a hard error today, not a deprecation warning.

The model-lifecycle arc keeps producing the same lesson: retirements now change the API shape, not just the model string. Grep your codebase for generate_images() before your first incident ticket does it for you.
