The slow-down essay stopped being an essay: Altman and Musk endorsed it, and the AI complex sold off on three continents

On 12 September Anthropic's Dario Amodei published a roughly 3,800-word essay whose operative sentence was "We must slow the pace at which we improve the capabilities of AI models." Edition 086 carded it. Edition 087 demoted it to Tracking for producing no second-day development. It produced one on day three. Over the weekend Sam Altman and Elon Musk each posted on X that they agree with him, and on Monday 14 September the trade opened against the entire AI capital-expenditure complex. In premarket, Marvell, SK Hynix, Micron and Super Micro were each off about 6%, CoreWeave, SanDisk, Intel and AMD off more than 4%, and Nvidia and Broadcom sliding about 3% after the open (Yahoo Finance). By the session, one wrap had Intel down 7% and AMD down 6% with Nvidia pulling back (24/7 Wall St). It was not a US story: SK Hynix closed down more than 6% and Samsung more than 4% in Seoul, SoftBank fell roughly 10–11% in Tokyo, and in Europe ASML lost 5–6%, Infineon more than 7% and Nokia roughly 8% (CNN, FinancialJuice). Cybersecurity shares rallied while AI names sank (CNBC).

So what: the pacing argument now has a price, and the market has shown you which way it trades. If your 2027 plan assumes uninterrupted capability scaling and uninterrupted capex, you have just been handed a market-tested downside case — plus the first sector rotation that prices the alternative.

IMPACT 9/10#1 POLICY · GOVERNANCE

Anthropic, OpenAI and Google have been quietly meeting since July about building their own AI standards body

Reported by The Information and carried on 14 September by CNN: the three labs have held working-group meetings since July aimed at creating an industry-led AI safety standards body, and the group met as recently as last week. The idea has a public antecedent — Google DeepMind CEO Demis Hassabis proposed a FINRA-style, US-based Frontier AI Standards Body on 14 July, arguing it should test advanced models before deployment. Sam Altman reportedly told employees at an internal meeting that he supports a testing and auditing body but believes the major labs will have to build one themselves absent US government backing. The three differ on how much government belongs in it: Anthropic leans toward government partnership, OpenAI emphasises voluntary industry standards while backing specific state-level legislation (Quartz, InvestingLive). The talks predate Amodei's Saturday pacing essay — which is the point.

A private body with pre-deployment testing powers would become the de facto gate on frontier releases long before any statute does, and it would be written by the three companies it gates. If you build on frontier models, start tracking this the way you track the EU AI Act — and ask which of your vendors gets a vote.

IMPACT 8/10#2 MODELS · SAFETY

Stripping a model's refusals is now a hosted product you buy with a credit card

Dated 3 September and missed by this briefing until now: TechCrunch reports Abliteration.ai selling hosted access to open-weight models with their trained refusal behaviour removed — "abliteration" is the technique, and the company is named for it. The current base is Z.AI's GLM-5.3, queryable from a browser or through an API at $5 per million input or output tokens at the standard rate. The company said in a social post that its purpose is to enable "offensive cyber, red-teaming, and agent testing work other models refuse to do." TechCrunch opened a free browser account and reports the modified GLM-5.3 produced code to steal saved Chrome passwords and a detailed protocol involving a dangerous human pathogen. Identity verification does not extend beyond logging the payment card used (The Decoder).

This is the MiniMax H3 licence finding inverted: open weights are what make the safety layer detachable, and detachable means resellable. Assume a refusal-free hosted version of every open-weight model in your threat model already exists and is one card payment away — then re-read your abuse-detection assumptions, which were written for models that say no.

IMPACT 7/10#5 POLICY · CHINA

China's Supreme People's Court issued the first national judicial rules for AI disputes — five parts, 24 articles

On 7 September the Supreme People's Court released the Opinions on the Trial of Cases Involving Artificial Intelligence Disputes: five parts, 24 articles, the first set of AI adjudication rules from China's highest judicial body (People's Daily, China.org.cn). The core is personality rights: using a natural person's voice as training corpus to generate an identifiable synthetic voice, or generating and publishing an identifiable virtual likeness, without consent creates liability for infringement of name, portrait or voice rights — explicitly covering AI face-swapping, voice cloning and "AI resurrection of the deceased" (Global Times, SCMP). The Opinions are framed as guidance to lower courts applying existing law — Civil Code, PIPL, Cybersecurity Law, Data Security Law, Copyright Law, Anti-Unfair Competition Law — on the express premise that China has not enacted dedicated AI legislation (National Law Review, Data Compliance China). Commentary notes they sidestep the biggest open question: training-data copyright.

China now has operative AI liability rules ahead of an AI statute, and they bite hardest on synthetic likeness rather than on models. If you generate faces or voices for the Chinese market, consent provenance just became a litigable record rather than a policy preference — and note the shape of the move: courts first, legislation later, which is a template other jurisdictions can copy faster than they can legislate.

OpenClaw 2.0 turns the personal agent into a shared, handoffable session

IMPACT 6/10#12 RESEARCH · BENCHMARKS

NatureBench: the strongest coding agent beats published Nature-family SOTA on 17.8% of tasks

Edition 085 recorded NatureBench as "undated in results" while flagging the research beat as unrepresented. It has a date and a number now. arXiv:2606.24530 — a June 2026 preprint, currently at v2 — is a 90-task cross-discipline benchmark distilled from peer-reviewed Nature-family publications, built on NatureGym, an automated pipeline that constructs a standardised containerised environment per task to fix the environment-fragmentation problem that has limited the credibility of prior agent-on-research benchmarks. Across ten frontier agent configurations under a strict web-search-disabled protocol, the strongest surpasses published SOTA on only 17.8% of tasks under the g>0.1 criterion. The authors' pathway analysis finds agents succeed mainly through methodological translation — converting a scientific task into a familiar supervised prediction problem — rather than scientific invention. Six domains: cellular omics, protein biology, biomedical modelling, physical modelling, molecular design, relational reasoning. Code and leaderboard are public (GitHub, HF Papers).

The same finding as yesterday's AgentActionBench, approached from the other end: agents can run the pipeline, not originate the method. Use 17.8% as the honest ceiling the next time someone pitches you an autonomous research agent.
