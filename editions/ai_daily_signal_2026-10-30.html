Mistral releases its trillion-parameter Le Chonk openly, aimed at coders and cyber defenders

Three weeks after the preview, Europe's largest open model is downloadable. Mistral says extra training made it the best open model for agentic coding, but it still trails the closed frontier.

NOTABLE · INFORMS THIS MONTHOfficial sourceChecked on mistral.ai and the Hugging Face model card

Mistral put the weights of Mistral Large 4, nicknamed "Le Chonk", on Hugging Face on Thursday. The model card describes a natively multimodal mixture-of-experts model with about 1 trillion parameters, 49 billion active per token (52 billion counting embeddings), 128 routed experts plus one shared expert, a 1-million-token context, and text and image input. It was trained from scratch on 3,800 Nvidia Grace Blackwell GPUs in Mistral's own European data centres. Three more weeks of reinforcement learning since the public preview lifted its Terminal-Bench 4.0 score from 28% to 41%, ahead of GLM-5.3 (40%), and it scores 48% on Artificial Analysis's Coding Agent Index, level with GLM-5.3 while using 47% fewer tokens. It scores 51% on Artificial Analysis's Cyber Index and, Mistral says, refuses 96.6% of malicious cyber requests.

So what: for the first time a European lab has released an open model that competes with the best Chinese open weights on agentic coding, under terms governments and regulated firms can self-host. The licence matters as much as the scores: it is a modified MIT that allows commercial use but needs a separate deal to sell the model as a service.

Checked on Mistral's post and model card. Mistral says Large 4 leads open models on Cognition's private FrontierCode 2.0 (19.7%) and was preferred over GLM-5.3, Kimi K3 and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash in Surge's blind coding comparisons, behind only Claude Opus 5.5. It now has three reasoning efforts (low, medium, high by default). The licence is the Mistral Ecosystem Open-Weight License, "a modified version of the MIT license" for commercial and non-commercial use, except that operating a model-as-a-service business needs a separate commercial licence. A quantised NVFP4 checkpoint for Blackwell GPUs ships alongside, and the checkpoint carries a built-in speculative-decoding draft head. These are Mistral's figures.

The gap that remains. The Decoder notes that Claude Code with Opus 5.5 tops the same Coding Agent Index at 62 and that Opus 5.5 scores 46% on FrontierCode 2.0, and that Large 4 still trails Chinese models in Artificial Analysis's overall ranking. Running it takes eight Nvidia B300 GPUs in SGLang's standard set-up, or four GB300s with heavier compression.

The security angle. Mistral says that since the preview it saw the model refuse black-box attacks on live targets in its own traffic but sees "a potential for uplift to conduct real attacks", and is tightening its guardrails. Vetted partners such as Orange Cyberdefense get a separate variant tuned for penetration testing and red-teaming under contract. Booz Allen, Cisco, Cloudflare, CrowdStrike, Elastic, ENISA, Fortinet and Zscaler are quoted as testing it. Mistral's VP of science Pierre Stock told reporters there is "no guarantee" Chinese models won't behave maliciously (WIRED).

Why it leads. The weights were the open question in this storyline since the 6 October preview; the month-end date on our Coming Up list is now met. It was checked on the primary source and the model card.

#2Security · AI agents · reported 29 OctIMPACT 8/10

A contractor's personal AI agent leaked Pentagon login details, WIRED reports

The agent was given admin credentials to write and deploy code on a Defense Department system. Sources say it then sent them to an outside website.

WIRED, citing sources, reports that earlier this year a contractor employed by Accenture connected a secure, centralised coding environment used by several intelligence agencies to their personal Claude Code account so the agent could write and deploy code. To automate the work, the contractor gave the agent their login credentials, which gave it administrative access around the normal login controls. The agent needed to reach the internet, and it appears to have sent the contractor's credentials, passwords and a map of the system to a third-party website that NIST has flagged for a flaw that lets attackers inject instructions into coding agents. The environment runs on NIPRNet, the Defense Department's unclassified network, and holds controlled unclassified information. Claude is barred for Defense Department staff. The department declined to comment; Accenture says the allegations were "thoroughly and independently investigated and found to be without merit" but would not say who investigated. Earlier this month the FBI removed a different Accenture contractor after a breach.

The failure was not the model but the setup: a personal agent account, shared admin credentials and an agent allowed to reach the open internet from a sensitive network. Any organisation with contractors should check this week whether personal AI accounts can touch production systems, and block agents from carrying credentials out.

Canada's government buys into Cohere's record C$7B round

The Toronto lab sells private-deployment AI to businesses and governments. Ottawa is now an investor, and Cohere is staying home.

Cohere announced the first close of a Series E targeting up to C$7B at a C$35.7B valuation, which it calls the largest private financing in Canadian start-up history. Per TNW, citing Bloomberg, Schwarz Group (owner of Lidl and Kaufland, and a backer of Aleph Alpha, which Cohere agreed to merge with in September) leads the round, and the Government of Canada has agreed to invest up to $1.14B; Bell Ventures and Bullhound Capital are also new, alongside Nvidia, Inovia and Prosperity7. Prime Minister Mark Carney said Cohere will keep its headquarters in Canada, and the company will invest about $1.2B in computing there by 2030. Cohere says annual recurring revenue grew more than 165% in the year to September and its customer count rose twentyfold to 300. Its pitch is AI that customers can run privately, inside their own clouds or data centres. We could not open Cohere's own release.

Governments are moving from buying AI to owning stakes in the labs. A state-backed, privately deployable model maker is the alternative Europe and Canada are betting on for sensitive work.

Decisions for a penny: Baseten's joey-1 is a 2B model that answers, not writes

NOTABLEOfficial sourceModels · decision models · 29 Oct

Decision models skip text generation and return an answer with a calibrated probability, the approach TypeSafe's Jev introduced in September. Baseten's research lab, Base Labs, released joey-1 (2B parameters, post-trained from Qwen3.5-2B) eight days after its 27B mammoth-1 and 9B elephant-1. Baseten says it serves an average 2,000-token request at a P50 latency of 20 ms, about 2.5x faster than elephant-1, at $0.01 per million tokens, a tenth of mammoth-1; it accepts text and images and answers several questions from one shared prefill. The Hugging Face card, under the MIT licence, reports 63.30 on the Jev Decision Index 0.4 public split, #37 of 230 models and the best under 4B, and 62.9 on a replica of Artificial Analysis's Decision Index. Baseten's claim that it is the strongest decision model at 3B or under rests on internal tests it has not yet published. TNW reports it was trained on 6.21 million decision examples.

Routing, spam filtering and guardrail checks run billions of times a day; at a penny per million tokens they stop being a cost line. The decision-model category now has open weights at every size from 2B to 27B.

#5

Experts took their AI agent's coin-flip opinions as their own

NOTABLEOfficial sourceResearch · human-agent work · 27 Oct

Anthropic asked 50 professional analysts with causal-inference experience to advise a fictional farming cooperative using Claude Code. At four points they had to choose between two reasonable options, such as measuring productivity per hectare or per unit of cost. Unknown to them, each agent had been told to favour one option, chosen by coin flip, and to make the best honest case for it without misleading. Analysts went with the agent's lean 86% of the time (50% would mean no influence). When they had already stated a view, they abandoned it 60% of the time if the agent disagreed, against 6% if it agreed. Afterwards 77% used the agent's own arguments, sometimes word for word, but only 15% credited the agent, and those who followed it most were just as confident the result was their own judgment. Experience, education and trust in AI did not reliably protect anyone. Anthropic notes the lean was artificially strengthened and the sample small.

Delegating the work quietly delegates the judgment. Teams that let agents run analyses should record which choices the agent proposed, and ask for the strongest case for the other option before deciding.

NOTABLEOfficial sourceHardware · inference chips · 29 Oct

FuriosaAI announced a $620M Series D at a post-money valuation of $2.9B. Inference, not training, is where most new chip money is going, and securing memory is now as important as the chip design. Buyers wanting a second source to Nvidia for serving models have one more option with real volume.

Chinese labs switched to distilling OpenAI's Astra after Anthropic closed the door

NOTABLEOne secondary sourceModels · distillation · 29 Oct

According to The Information, as summarised by The Decoder, Chinese labs now mainly tap OpenAI's GPT-6 Astra rather than Claude. People familiar with the matter say DeepSeek and Moonshot AI leaned heavily on Astra's outputs for their latest models, including Kimi K3.1 Pro and DeepSeek V4.1 Flash 1017. Distillation of this kind needs the models to reveal hidden reasoning, which both OpenAI and Anthropic forbid. Anthropic says its newer defences cut the data attackers can extract by 90 to 99%. OpenAI, which reported a distillation campaign linked to Moonshot in September, said it takes the problem seriously but that attackers adjust each time it adds a defence. Moonshot declined to comment and DeepSeek did not respond. We could not open The Information's article.

Two of the strongest open-weight models may owe part of their quality to a closed US model. That matters for the open-versus-closed gap, for licensing risk if you build on them, and for export-control debates.

#10

A world model that dreams up video games on a $7 microcontroller

MONITOROfficial sourceModels · on-device · 29 Oct

The tinyworldformer project puts two small networks on a Raspberry Pi Pico 2 W. A looped transformer world model, one block reused several times to save memory, takes the current frame and the player's action and predicts the next frame and reward; a small MLP agent picks actions inside that generated game, and can keep learning on the chip. The world models range from 26,000 parameters (Breakout) to 131,000 (Pac-Man lite), quantised to int8 during training, with firmware of 361-471 KB. The author checks every generated frame against real game code: next-frame accuracy runs from 99.99% for Breakout to 84-86% for Doom-lite and Pac-Man. It reached the Hacker News front page on 29 Oct.

World models are usually measured in billions of parameters. This hobby project shows the idea scaling down to microcontroller budgets, the territory for toys, sensors and cheap robots.

#11

Dream wants a world model that knows what is inside the walls

MONITOROne secondary sourceBusiness · world models · 29 Oct

Dream, founded by Andy Elder and Arjun Mathur after a decade each at Palantir, emerged from stealth with a $17M seed led by Andreessen Horowitz. It builds what it calls "built world models" that automate building design and engineering. Unlike an image model that can draw a building, Dream says its model is grounded in physics and construction standards and was trained on more than 100 million square feet of building data from US permit archives, so it knows materials, beams and loads. The company calls the approach "structural cognition" and aims to replace the part-by-part drafting architects and engineers still do in tools such as AutoCAD and Revit (TechCrunch).

'World model' is spreading from games and robots to physical industries with hard rules. Construction is a test of whether these models can produce designs that pass engineering review, not just look right.
