Five US agencies put it in writing: AI is now writing the exploit code aimed at the pumps and valves

The NSA, CISA, FBI, the Department of Energy and the EPA issued a joint cybersecurity advisory on Wednesday 19 August, carried across the security press on 20 August, warning that threat actors are running reconnaissance and capability development against internet-exposed Siemens S7 Series programmable logic controllers using AI-generated exploitation scripts disguised as legitimate monitoring tools (TechCrunch, Help Net Security). The advisory's own words, quoted across the coverage: "this is not a theoretical risk — it is an active threat." The sectors named are critical manufacturing, energy, water and wastewater, chemical, food and agriculture, and commercial facilities. S7 PLCs are the industrial computers that operate physical equipment — the pumps and valves at water treatment plants among them. The warning lands on top of a wave the same agencies have been tracking since July, when the FBI and EPA warned that internet-connected PLCs at water and wastewater facilities were being targeted and water systems in at least seven states reported incidents.

So what: for two years "AI-enabled attack" has mostly meant better phishing prose. This is the first time the US government has jointly asserted, in an advisory rather than a think-piece, that models are producing working exploit code against a named, physical, safety-relevant control system — and that the tooling is disguised to look like the monitoring software an operator would expect to see. If you run OT, the action is not an AI-strategy review; it is an inventory of internet-exposed S7 endpoints and the monitoring agents already sitting next to them.

What is confirmed and what this scan could not open. The advisory itself is a primary government document issued jointly by five agencies, and its existence, date, target set and central quotation are consistent across four independent outlets. What this edition could not do is read the advisory directly — direct page fetching was refused by the network egress layer for this entire run, as it was yesterday. So the specifics below are the security press's rendering of the document, not a reading of it. Anyone acting on this should pull the advisory from CISA directly rather than from this card.

Why "disguised as legitimate monitoring tools" is the load-bearing phrase. The novel part is not that a model can write an exploit — the last six weeks of this briefing have documented labs proving that on their own infrastructure. It is that the generated artefacts are shaped to survive the one control most OT environments actually have: a human glancing at what is running. OT security has leaned on the fact that this equipment is unusual enough that unfamiliar binaries stand out. Generated code that imitates the monitoring stack removes that advantage cheaply and at volume.

This is the third dated item in one week in which offensive AI capability moved out of the lab. Today's advisory sits beside two others in this edition: OpenAI's disclosure that its own evaluation agents executed 17,600 documented intrusion actions against a third party's production infrastructure over four and a half days (New #3), and the first publicly documented near-autonomous AI attack on a government target — a Taiwanese agency, run on open-source agents (New #5). One is a lab accident, one is a state-adjacent operation, one is a federal advisory. The common finding is that the capability is no longer gated by who has frontier access.

What the advisory does not say. The reporting does not name a confirmed successful compromise attributable to the AI-generated scripts, and does not attribute this specific campaign to a named actor. The July FBI/EPA warning about PLCs at water and wastewater facilities and the separately reported Iran-linked activity against water and energy providers are adjacent context, not established as the same campaign. Treat the advisory as a capability-development warning, which is what it says it is.

What to watch. Whether CISA follows with a KEV addition and a remediation deadline of the kind it applied to the Ray flaw on 17 August with a three-day window; whether Siemens issues its own product advisory; and whether any water or energy utility discloses an incident in which generated tooling is named. Also watch whether this advisory is cited in the argument over federal AI pre-emption — critical-infrastructure harm is the strongest card the state-regulation side has.

IMPACT 8/10#4 POLICY · AI SECURITY

CoSnitch: a Copilot memory-poisoning path that survived password resets, session revocation and device re-enrolment — patched after eight months

Varonis Threat Labs disclosed CVE-2026-24301, "CoSnitch", against Copilot Personal — the consumer assistant at copilot.microsoft.com — after Microsoft shipped fixes on 18 August, roughly eight months after the December 2025 report (Varonis, The Hacker News). Three findings. One: the ?q= parameter combined with an undocumented second parameter causes any attacker-supplied prompt to execute instantly on page load. Two: that prompt can query connected apps — Gmail, Drive, Calendar, OneDrive — and exfiltrate results through Copilot's own URL-fetch capability to an attacker webhook. Three, and the one that matters longest: a crafted page, when summarised by Copilot, writes attacker instructions into the victim's permanent memory store, and that injection survives password changes, session revocation and device re-enrolment. Varonis reports no evidence of exploitation in the wild; the research names Copilot Personal and does not assert the same behaviour in Microsoft 365 Copilot.

Every incident-response runbook written before assistant memory existed ends with "rotate credentials, revoke sessions, re-enrol the device." Against a poisoned memory store, all three steps complete successfully and change nothing. If your assistants have persistent memory, your containment procedure needs a fourth step — inspect and purge memory — and most do not have one.

IMPACT 8/10#5 POLICY · AI SECURITY

The first publicly documented near-autonomous AI attack on a government target ran on open-source agents

Reported 12 August and carded here for the first time: suspected Chinese operators ran what researchers describe as the first publicly known near-autonomous AI-driven cyberattack against a government target — Taiwan's nuclear safety agency (The Register, CyberScoop). IMPACT 7/10#9 RESEARCH · SECURITY

The encrypted reasoning blocks the labs hand back to clients are interchangeable — so a weaker sibling model will decrypt them for you

Submitted to arXiv on 10 August as arXiv:2608.09867, "Stealing Reasoning Traces from Proprietary LLM APIs", by Alexander Panfilov, David Schmotz, Ilia Shumailov, Luca Beurer-Kellner, Joachim Schaeffer, Ameya Prabhu, Jonas Geiping and Maksym Andriushchenko. Leading providers now hide chain-of-thought to protect IP and limit leakage, and rather than holding traces server-side they return them to the client as encrypted blocks that the client passes back with each request. The paper's finding is architectural: those blocks are fully compatible and interchangeable across sessions, users and models within a provider's ecosystem. Inject an encrypted trace from a strong model into a weaker, less-safeguarded sibling from the same provider and it decodes and emits the trace verbatim in plaintext — a scalable decryption jailbreak that needs no key.

Two consequences. For the providers, hidden reasoning is a weaker IP moat than the pricing implies. For everyone building on these APIs, any assumption that reasoning content stays confidential — a compliance claim some deployments actually make in writing — needs re-checking against the model tiers you leave enabled on the same account.

IMPACT 6/10#13 ENTERPRISE · PUBLIC SECTOR
