NVIDIA will underwrite $105 billion for one OpenAI data centre — and the chips are now the collateral

NVIDIA said on Monday 17 August that it will back as much as $105 billion in financing for the PORTS-Pike Technology Campus, an OpenAI data-centre site in Ohio, and separately invest $1.5 billion into SB Energy, the SoftBank unit developing it (Bloomberg, CNBC). SB Energy builds and owns the campus; OpenAI leases it for 20 years. First phase is 4.25 gigawatts of compute with an option on another 3.75GW. In return OpenAI committed to using NVIDIA chips exclusively on the site, and Jensen Huang put OpenAI's broader infrastructure plans at roughly $600 billion of NVIDIA compute through 2030. Read it against the other half of the same week: on 10 August NVIDIA announced financing platforms with Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR to mobilise over $500 billion of third-party capital for AI compute, and by 14 August Goldman was reported marketing the structure to investors.

So what: this is the week the buildout stopped being funded out of hyperscaler cash flow and started being funded like infrastructure debt — SPVs, 20-year leases, GPUs as collateral. That changes who is exposed when utilisation disappoints: not just the labs, but pension money. If you buy compute, your counterparty risk now runs through a capital structure, not a balance sheet.

The structure, as reported. NVIDIA is not building the campus and is not the landlord. SB Energy — SoftBank's energy arm — builds and owns PORTS-Pike; OpenAI signs a 20-year lease; NVIDIA backstops up to $105 billion of the financing that gets it built, and separately puts $1.5 billion of equity into SB Energy itself. The consideration flowing back to NVIDIA is an exclusivity commitment: OpenAI uses NVIDIA silicon on that site. Initial capacity is 4.25GW with a 3.75GW expansion option, which would put the fully-built campus at 8GW — larger than the entire announced first phase of most sovereign AI programmes.

Why the $500 billion platform is the same story. On 10 August NVIDIA announced it was establishing AI compute infrastructure financing platforms with six of the largest alternative and traditional asset managers — Apollo, BlackRock, Blackstone, Brookfield, Goldman Sachs and KKR — explicitly to mobilise more than $500 billion of third-party capital. The mechanism is that compute is treated as a financeable, revenue-generating asset: capital deployed through private offerings and bonds issued by special-purpose entities, with GPU fleets as the underlying collateral. Huang's framing to CNBC was that his chips are an "investable asset". By 14 August Goldman was reported to be in talks with investors on the Ohio-scale piece specifically. The Ohio deal is the first very large worked example of the platform's logic.

The circularity question, named. Coverage has already put the obvious objection to Huang: NVIDIA is financing a customer's purchase of NVIDIA chips, in exchange for a commitment to buy NVIDIA chips. He has rejected the "circular financing" characterisation, arguing the demand is real and pointing at the ~$600B compute figure through 2030. This briefing is not in a position to adjudicate that — but the disclosure to watch is whether any of this appears as vendor financing, guarantee or off-balance-sheet exposure in NVIDIA's Q2 filing.

What is not established. The $105B is a maximum backed amount, not cash deployed today, and the reported terms — how much is guarantee versus direct investment, the seniority, the drawdown schedule — are not public. Neither is the split between the two announcements: whether Ohio draws on the six-manager platform or sits alongside it. All four sources here are secondary; direct fetches of nvidianews.nvidia.com, openai.com and cnbc.com were blocked by this environment's egress proxy, so nothing in this card was read off the primary release.

Watch next. NVIDIA's Q2 earnings on 26 August are now the single most load-bearing date on this radar: the first scheduled opportunity to see how any of this is booked, how the SpaceX and AMD Helios commitments sit alongside it, and whether management quantifies the financing exposure. Also watch whether the ratings agencies say anything about GPU-collateralised paper, and whether the data-centre IPO queue behind Vantage reprices on it.

IMPACT 6/10#5 MODELS · OPEN WEIGHTS

Tuesday's deep beat: NVIDIA's Nemotron 3.5 Lightning ships weights, data and recipes under OpenMDW-1.1

Released 11 August: Nemotron 3.5 Lightning, a 30B mixture-of-experts model with 3B active parameters per token, distilled from Nemotron 3 Ultra and aimed at high-volume agentic workloads (CNBC, SiliconANGLE). NVIDIA claims up to 4x faster output and roughly 30% faster agentic task completion against comparable open models. It runs on a single consumer GPU — RTX PCs or DGX Spark — and ships under the permissive OpenMDW-1.1 licence with weights, training data and recipes, downloadable without requesting permission. NVIDIA also released NeMo Switchyard, an open library routing agent requests to the cheapest adequate model. OCI added support the same day.

Against a month of staged weights and revenue-share licences — GLM-5.3 still undated, Qwen3.8-Max under a bespoke licence — a chip vendor just published the full recipe. The open-weight norm is being reset by the party with the least reason to protect model margin.

Day 32: the buildout stops being self-funded and becomes a credit product

WHAT CHANGED Escalated on financing structure, not capacity. In eight days the arc moved from "who is buying GPUs" to "who is lending against them": NVIDIA established $500B-plus third-party capital platforms with six asset managers on 10 August, Goldman was reported marketing them on 14 August, and on 17 August NVIDIA backed up to $105B for a single OpenAI campus that SoftBank's SB Energy will own and OpenAI will lease for 20 years.
The arc's existing components all still stand and now read as the demand side of a credit story: ONEOK's 1GW gas supply agreement with 40-plus counterparties in the pipeline; SMIC's first $3B quarter at 93.7% utilisation with a warning that Q3 wafers cost more; and the data-centre listing queue with Vantage's exploratory ~$100B process reported ahead of CyrusOne and DayOne. What is new is that the marginal dollar is now institutional third-party capital collateralised on GPUs, deployed through SPVs — which imports a different failure mode. A demand air pocket used to compress a hyperscaler's margin; now it also has to be explained to bondholders.

Model your compute counterparty as a capital structure, not a company. And treat NVIDIA's 26 August Q2 as the read on this arc — it is the first scheduled disclosure that could size the guarantee exposure.
