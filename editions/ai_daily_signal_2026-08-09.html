The data centre leaves the planet — and NVIDIA gets the whole thing to itself

SpaceX has committed exclusively to NVIDIA for its AI compute, on the ground and in orbit. The two companies are co-designing the computing payload for Starmind AI1, a satellite built to run AI workloads in space on NVIDIA's next-generation Rubin GPUs and Vera CPUs, announced across 4–7 August (Tech Startups, Forbes). Musk framed the exclusivity on the earnings call; NVIDIA becomes the sole accelerator supplier for a company that already owns xAI and Cursor. The number that makes it more than a demo is the filing: SpaceX has asked the FCC for authority to deploy up to one million orbital data-centre satellites between 500 and 2,000 km, described as a "petabit laser mesh" (Motley Fool, HotHardware). First Starmind launches are slated for next year; full data-centre satellites late 2027. It lands the same week AMD bought a company that etches models into silicon (New #2) and Anthropic admitted it is designing its own chips (New #3) — three separate answers, in seventy-two hours, to the same question about where inference is going to run.

So what: after eleven editions in which the compute constraint was zoning boards, grid interconnects and memory supply, the loudest players are now routing around the constraint rather than queuing for it — one into orbit, two into custom silicon. Nothing here changes your 2026 capacity plan, but if your 2028 assumption is "GPUs in a leased rack at market rates," that assumption now has three well-capitalised counter-examples.

What was announced. NVIDIA has agreed to supply the accelerators for SpaceX's Starmind orbital AI data centres, and SpaceX said it will use NVIDIA architecture exclusively going forward for both ground and orbital compute. The first article is Starmind AI1, a satellite whose computing payload SpaceX and NVIDIA designed together, running Rubin GPUs and Vera CPUs — the same two parts NVIDIA put into full production earlier this year. Launches of Starmind satellites are described as beginning next year, with the first full data-centre satellites late in 2027 (Tech Startups, Motley Fool).

The filing is the story. A single AI satellite is a press release. An FCC application for authority to deploy up to one million orbital data-centre satellites between 500 and 2,000 km altitude, linked by what SpaceX calls a "petabit laser mesh," is a stated intention to build a compute tier that no terrestrial permitting authority can gate. Read against this briefing's own compute thread — Brookfield and NextEra's ~$100B on the Paducah site, FERC's six show-cause orders on large-load interconnection due 17 August, a Milltown survey finding nearly half of US voters back a temporary data-centre construction ban — orbit is a direct answer to the objection that terrestrial siting has become the binding constraint.

Why it is the lead over the silicon stories. AMD's Taalas purchase and Anthropic's chip team both make inference cheaper inside the existing model of where compute lives. This one proposes a different place for it to live, and attaches a sole-supplier commitment from the largest private launch operator to the largest accelerator vendor. It is also the first item in eleven editions to give the hardware beat a lead, after yesterday produced no hardware card at all.

The caveats, stated plainly. No first-party NVIDIA or SpaceX newsroom post was reachable in this scan — the network egress proxy blocked direct page fetches, so every figure here rests on trade and business coverage of the announcement and of Musk's earnings-call remarks rather than on primary text read directly. "Up to one million satellites" is the ceiling SpaceX asked for, not a deployment plan; the FCC has neither granted nor scheduled it. And the exclusivity is a statement of intent from a company whose CEO has reversed supplier commitments before.

Whether NVIDIA or SpaceX publishes a first-party technical description of the Starmind payload; whether the FCC dockets the constellation application; NVIDIA's Q2 earnings on 26 August, which is the first scheduled opportunity to size the commitment; and whether AMD's Taalas deal draws a counter-bid for orbital or edge inference silicon.

IMPACT 7/10#2 HARDWARE · M&A

AMD buys Taalas — a chip company whose product is the model, burned into the silicon

Announced 6 August: AMD is acquiring Taalas, a Toronto inference-silicon startup founded in 2023 by former Tenstorrent and AMD architects, whose approach hardwires a trained model's weights directly into the chip rather than streaming them from memory — AMD's stated rationale is removing the compute and memory bottlenecks of general-purpose architectures (AMD Newsroom, CNBC, The Register). Terms were not disclosed; the deal is expected to close in Q4 2026 subject to regulatory approval. Taalas technology is to be folded into the accelerator roadmap alongside Instinct, EPYC and ROCm.

This is the same thesis as OLIX's no-HBM design, bought rather than built: the next round of inference competition is specialised architecture, not faster general-purpose GPUs. It also implies a model you can no longer swap — worth asking what a re-fab cycle costs when the weights are the wafer.

IMPACT 7/10#3 HARDWARE · LABS

Anthropic confirms an in-house chip design team, aiming to halve inference cost

On 5 August Anthropic publicly confirmed a proprietary hardware programme it had been assembling quietly since at least early June: an in-house team co-designing silicon and Claude models together, targeting roughly 50% cuts in per-token inference cost (DCD, TechTimes). The company framed it as "the latest step in our multi-chip approach" and said it will keep a diversified stack — NVIDIA and AMD GPUs, Google TPUs, AWS Trainium. Chip-engineer roles are posted at $320,000–$485,000; Clive Chan, who joined in early June and was the second hardware hire on OpenAI's chip team after Tesla's Dojo programme, anchors the technical leadership. Samsung is reported as a manufacturing partner (Tom's Hardware) — reported, not confirmed by either company.

If a lab can co-design its way to half the per-token cost, published API pricing stops being a proxy for the underlying economics — and every "we'll just switch providers on price" contingency plan gets less predictive, not more.

IMPACT 7/10#4 MODELS · PRICING

OpenAI removes the free-tier message cap and makes GPT-5.6 Luna the default

Announced 6 August, effective from the week of 10 August: OpenAI is retiring the roughly 10-messages-per-5-hours limit and giving free ChatGPT accounts unlimited text chats, with GPT-5.6 Luna replacing GPT-5.5 as the default model for Free and Go users (TechCrunch, MacRumors). IMPACT 5/10#9 POLICY · WORKFORCE

Gallup: the people being laid off are disproportionately the ones not using AI

A Gallup survey of more than 23,000 US workers fielded in February 2026 and reported in mid-June finds tech employees who rarely use AI carry an 18% predicted probability of layoff against 6% for regular users — a gap that holds after controlling for age, education, industry and time since layoff (Forbes, 19 Jun; Boston Globe, 18 Jun). 62% of laid-off workers were AI non-users, yet only 1% of respondents named AI or automation as the primary reason — the top answers were restructuring (15%), budget and cost-cutting (11%) and economic conditions (11%). Gallup separately reports ~21% of US employees seeing layoffs at their employer in Q1 2026 (Gallup). This is February data reported in June, carded here for the first time; it is not new this week.

It reframes the displacement argument: the visible mechanism is not "AI replaced the role" but "the restructuring kept whoever had already adopted." That is a much harder thing to legislate against — and a much easier thing to act on individually.

Three different numbers for AI-driven job cuts, and all three are in print

WHAT CHANGED PROMOTED from Tracking. A third figure enters the ledger: tracker data as of 8 August puts ~50,000 US job cuts this year explicitly linked to AI, about 17% of ~300,000 total, and 316,000+ since early 2023. That sits against the 87,714 Challenger-derived AI-attributed figure carded on 7 August and the 322 events / 205,832 workers event ledger, which is unchanged. The thread moved because the disagreement is now three-way and load-bearing.
The event-level ledger is flat: 322 layoff events affecting 205,832 workers as of 8 August, roughly 936 job losses a day (DisplaceIndex, SkillSyncer). The AI-attribution layer is not flat and does not reconcile: ~50,000 AI-linked cuts year-to-date on one count, 87,714 on the Challenger-derived count this briefing carried two days ago, and — from New #9 — only 1% of laid-off workers themselves naming AI or automation as the primary reason, against 15% naming restructuring. All three can be true simultaneously because they measure different things: employer-stated cause, analyst attribution, and worker perception. None of them is the same as displacement.

Stop quoting a single AI-layoffs number. If you need one for a board deck, quote the range and say which of the three questions it answers — the gap between 1% and 87,714 is the whole argument.
